## Graduate
For inclusion, adapting communication, or team culture, my first-choice story is the **Swing Dance beginner withdrawing** story.

I would use that because the issue was not formal diversity language. It was a real moment where people in the same room were experiencing the same activity differently. What I changed was the communication style. I quietly suggested to one experienced dancer that we give one correction at a time because if we gave three, the beginner would stop hearing all of them. Then when I danced with her, I simplified the language. I said, “Don’t worry about making it look like dancing yet. Just step, step, wait.” And when she apologised again, I said, “You don’t need to apologise every time. Everyone here is being wrong at different speeds.”

The proof that it helped is that her behaviour changed. She laughed properly, not the embarrassed laugh from before. She stopped apologising after every mistake, kept her steps going, and stayed in the rotation instead of stepping out to the wall. The corrections around her also became simpler and more useful.

So the action helped her stay engaged because I did not lower the standard or tell people to stop correcting. I changed the dose and language of the correction so she could keep participating long enough to improve.

My backup story would be the **newer crew member bagging** story.

That one is more work-based. A newer crew member was overwhelmed during a rush because every open bag looked equally urgent. I adapted my support by giving him the ready / waiting / unclear pattern instead of telling him to hurry up. So I would use Swing Dance if the question is about inclusion, different experience levels, or adapting communication. I would use the crew member story if the question is about developing someone in a work setting.

## Graduate
I would use that lesson by watching the person’s response to feedback, not just whether the feedback itself is correct.

On a frontline team, two associates can make the same mistake for different reasons. One person may understand the work but need direct correction. Another may be new, overloaded, or worried about making mistakes in front of others. If I use the same tone and amount of feedback for both, I may be technically right but practically ineffective.

So I would first try to diagnose what kind of problem it is. Is this a skill gap, a confidence issue, an overload issue, or a standards issue? If someone is new or under rush pressure, I would keep feedback short and specific: one correction at a time, clear language, and the next action they can take immediately.

I would also avoid embarrassing someone publicly unless there is an immediate safety or quality issue that has to be stopped. If it is coaching, I would correct the work calmly in the moment, then give fuller feedback after the pressure drops.

The thing I would watch afterward is whether the person stays engaged. Do they keep trying? Do they ask better questions? Do they repeat the same mistake less often? Do they use the pattern without prompting? That tells me whether my communication actually helped.

So for me, the lesson is not to lower the standard. It is to deliver the standard in a way the person can receive and act on. Once they can stay in the work, then I can raise the complexity and expectation gradually.

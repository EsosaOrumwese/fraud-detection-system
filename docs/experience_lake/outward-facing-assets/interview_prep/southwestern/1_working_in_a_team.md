## Situation

We were mobilised from SouthWestern’s Warri base to **Oben Field, OML 4, Edo State**, for a workover/recompletion job on an existing oil producer. The well had gone through the earlier workover phase, and the next activity was to run the production tubing/completion string back into the well, including the gas-lift components required for the completion.

Our SouthWestern crew was responsible for the tubular-running support: preparing the make-up equipment, rigging up the hydraulic power unit and tong package, supporting the production-tubing run, tracking the tally, recording job times, and preparing the field notes for the post-job report.

The crew included the field supervisor, a senior tubular-running operator, operator hands, two or three field engineers, logistics/offloading support, and myself as **Field Engineer & Operations Analytics Support**. My lane was the job tally, readiness checklist, equipment-status notes, and supporting the supervisor with clear job-status information during the operation.

On site, we were working around the rig crew, driller, HSE officer, company/client representative, and logistics team. The pre-job plan had already been discussed at base, but once we arrived at Oben, we still had to run the site toolbox talk, agree the work area, confirm the rig-up layout, and get everyone aligned before the completion string could be run.


## Problem

The issue appeared during the walkdown after the toolbox talk. The logistics team had offloaded the hydraulic power unit close to the easiest truck access point, and the operator hands had started laying the hose bundle from that position toward the tong area.

When I walked the route with the readiness checklist, the hose line was cutting across the access path between the pipe rack, V-door area, and the rig-floor approach. That was the same path the rig crew would use while preparing joints for pick-up. Part of the hose bundle also sat outside the boundary that HSE had marked during the toolbox talk.

The senior operator wanted to continue because the equipment was already partly coupled and the line could still reach the work area. One of the rig hands also felt the route could be managed by stepping over it and keeping people alert. HSE would not release the area with the hose crossing that access path, and the client representative was asking why the start was slipping when the crew had already completed the toolbox talk.

That created the real tension. Correcting the setup meant breaking part of the coupling, moving the hydraulic unit, rerouting the hoses, and rechecking the boundary. The people who had already done the physical work were more experienced than me, and they saw the delay as unnecessary. From my side of the job, the setup no longer matched the agreed work-area layout or the readiness checklist I was holding.

## Role

My task was to support the field supervisor during the pre-job readiness check. I had the readiness checklist and job-status notes, so I walked the setup after the toolbox talk and checked whether the actual rig-up matched the agreed work-area layout.

In this situation, my responsibility was to check the hydraulic unit position, hose route, access path, HSE boundary, and equipment-readiness status before the crew moved into the live operation. I also had to speak with the senior operator, HSE officer, logistics support, and rig-side contact so the supervisor could get one clear picture of what was ready, what was blocked, and what had to be changed.

Because the senior operator and rig hands had more field experience than me, I had to handle the issue carefully. My role was to bring the facts from the walkdown to the supervisor without making it sound like I was overruling the people who had done the physical setup.

## Action

I started by walking the hose route again instead of arguing from the checklist. I stood at the point where the hose crossed the access path and watched how the rig crew were moving between the pipe rack, V-door area, and rig-floor approach.

I first spoke to the HSE officer and asked him to show me the exact part of the route he would not release. He pointed to the hose bundle sitting outside the marked boundary and crossing the walkway the crew would use during pick-up.

I then went to the senior operator, not in front of everyone, and asked what had already been coupled and what would have to be broken if we moved the hydraulic unit. He showed me the connected section, the hose length, and the quickest place to reposition the unit without disturbing the whole setup.

After that, I checked with logistics where the unit could realistically be moved, because they knew the ground condition and truck access better than I did. They pointed out a firmer position a few metres away that was less convenient for offloading but gave a cleaner hose route.

I brought those three facts to the field supervisor: HSE would not release the current route, the senior operator could decouple only one section rather than strip everything down, and logistics had identified a workable new position for the hydraulic unit.

The supervisor asked me to help reset the toolbox discussion. I kept my wording practical because the rig hands were already irritated. I did not present it as them doing the setup wrong. I said the current hose route would put the pick-up path and HSE boundary in conflict once the tubing movement started, and that the fastest way to avoid a stop-work later was to move the hydraulic unit now and reroute the hose before the job went live.

With the rig hands, I spoke in terms of movement and space: where people would walk, where the tubing would come through, and where the hose would sit after the move. With HSE, I confirmed the corrected boundary. With the senior operator, I confirmed the decoupling sequence. With the client representative, the supervisor gave the updated start position: short reset now, cleaner start afterwards.

Once everyone agreed, I helped the operator hands mark the new hose route, updated the readiness notes, and checked the route again with HSE before the equipment was released for the operation.

## Result

The hydraulic unit was moved to the firmer position logistics identified, and the operator hands rerouted the hose bundle along the agreed boundary instead of across the pick-up path. The senior operator only had to break one coupled section, so the reset was contained and did not turn into a full strip-down.

HSE walked the route again, checked the access path between the pipe rack, V-door area, and rig-floor approach, and released the area for the operation. The rig hands who had pushed back earlier helped with the reroute once they could see the hose would no longer sit in their movement path during tubing pick-up.

The client representative got a clear update from the field supervisor: the start had been held briefly to correct the rig-up layout before the job went live. That avoided a worse delay later, where HSE could have stopped the operation after the crew had already begun moving tubing.

The completion run started with the service crew, rig crew, HSE, logistics, and client representative working from the same setup. For me, the strongest result was that the senior operator continued involving me in the readiness checks after that point. I had not won trust by arguing with more experienced people; I had earned it by checking their constraints, bringing the facts together, and helping the supervisor get the job moving safely.

## Learning

I learned that when experienced people push back, it is important to separate pride from practical constraint. The senior operator and rig hands were not resisting because they did not care about safety. They had already done part of the physical setup, and from their view the hose route still looked workable.

I also learned that adapting communication style means using the language each group works with. HSE responded to boundary and release conditions. The senior operator responded to what had to be decoupled and how much work would be lost. Logistics responded to ground position and equipment movement. The rig hands responded to where people would walk during pick-up. Once I spoke to each group through the part of the job they actually controlled, the reset became easier to accept.

The biggest lesson was that trust with more experienced people is built by checking the ground truth before speaking broadly. If I had gone straight into the toolbox reset saying the setup was wrong, I would have sounded like a junior person quoting a checklist. By walking the route, asking what had already been coupled, checking the release condition with HSE, and finding a workable move with logistics, I gave the supervisor facts he could use and gave the crew a practical way forward.

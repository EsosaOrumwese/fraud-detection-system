# Supporting Statement Posture

## Mandatory Reading Instruction

This document must be read as a connected reasoning method, not as a set of isolated tips, a generic prompt template, or a bank of phrases to copy.

The point is not to produce a polished personal statement. The point is to build a truthful selection argument from the job advert, the person specification, the candidate's real evidence, and any small evidence slices that can honestly be created before applying.

Any agent or reviewer using this document must preserve the sequence:

1. Read the job advert and person specification as the marking scheme.
2. Build the candidate evidence profile before drafting.
3. Map evidence to criteria and panel concerns.
4. Identify direct fit, adjacent fit, and non-claimable gaps.
5. Decide the argument for shortlisting.
6. Create or verify claim-bearing evidence slices where needed.
7. Draft a grounded supporting statement that reduces selection risk.

Do not jump straight to writing.

## What This Method Is For

This posture is for Civil Service, CPS, NHS, public-sector, charity, university, regulated-sector, and similar applications where a supporting statement, personal statement, behaviour answer, or cover-letter-style answer is sifted against defined criteria.

It is especially useful when:

- the role has a detailed person specification;
- the panel scores against essential and desirable criteria;
- behaviours, values, or competency frameworks matter;
- the candidate is a stretch but plausible applicant;
- the candidate has transferable evidence rather than exact job-title experience;
- the risk of sounding generic or inflated is high.

The method was developed through a deliberately difficult training exercise: using a job where the candidate was not an obvious direct match, then working through how a believable stretch candidate could build a grounded supporting statement without inventing experience.

The lesson from that exercise is:

> A strong supporting statement does not invent experience. It converts real or newly created evidence into job-relevant proof. It uses situations that are believable for the candidate's level, environment, and access. Where evidence is missing, the candidate can create honest portfolio evidence before applying, rather than pretending they already had it.

## Core Principle

A CV says:

> This is the shape of my fit.

A supporting statement says:

> Given what you need, given what I have actually done, and given where my fit is direct versus adjacent, here is why appointing me is a sensible, low-risk decision for this specific role.

That is the argument.

The statement is not:

- a longer version of the CV;
- a generic enthusiasm letter;
- a technical report;
- a list of criteria answered one by one;
- a polished story built from weak evidence;
- a place to pretend direct experience that does not exist.

The statement is a selection argument. Its job is to reduce the panel's doubt.

## The Hidden Hiring Question

Before writing, ask:

> Why does this employer need this person?

Do not stop at the duties. Find the problem underneath the advert.

Examples:

- A data role may not be buying "Python." It may be buying trustworthy reporting, controlled data use, and decision support.
- A healthcare analyst role may not be buying "dashboards." It may be buying governed analytics in a setting where poor data can affect service decisions.
- A CPS or Civil Service role may not be buying "good communication." It may be buying someone who can make defensible decisions, act with integrity, work within standards, and explain judgment clearly.

Once the real hiring problem is clear, the statement can answer that problem instead of sounding like a keyword match.

## The Job Advert Is The Marking Scheme

The first task is to extract the scoring surface.

Read the advert for:

- essential criteria;
- desirable criteria;
- duties;
- person specification;
- behaviours;
- values;
- technical skills;
- security, governance, confidentiality, or ethics requirements;
- stakeholder and communication expectations;
- evidence of pressure, pace, autonomy, or collaboration;
- hidden priorities implied by repeated wording.

Then classify the criteria.

### Green

Direct, strong fit. These should carry the statement.

Examples:

- tools used directly;
- relevant projects;
- matching technical methods;
- same kind of outputs;
- similar regulated or operational environment;
- actual stakeholder-facing evidence.

### Amber

Transferable or adjacent fit. These should be framed honestly.

Examples:

- adjacent sector experience;
- similar data but not the exact domain;
- academic or portfolio evidence rather than workplace evidence;
- part-time or operational evidence that supports behaviours rather than technical claims.

### Red

Not claimable yet. These should not be pretended.

Examples:

- direct sector experience that does not exist;
- tools only seen in a course but not used practically;
- formal stakeholder ownership that did not happen;
- leadership, policy, governance, or regulatory responsibility that was not actually held.

The statement should lead with Green, use Amber carefully, and avoid false Red claims.

## Candidate Profile Comes Before The Statement

Do not begin by drafting paragraphs.

First build the candidate profile.

Include:

- current work;
- previous work;
- academic projects;
- portfolio builds;
- internships;
- society or volunteer work;
- part-time work;
- technical assets;
- dashboards, reports, notebooks, SQL, validation logs, presentations, handover notes;
- operational or customer-facing evidence;
- communication and pressure examples;
- examples of values, integrity, judgment, and procedure-following.

The candidate profile grounds the statement. It stops the answer from becoming generic because the writing is attached to real work objects.

For digital, data, and technology roles in or around government, use the Civil Service DDaT Capability Framework as a reference while building the profile:

https://ddat-capability-framework.service.gov.uk/

Use it to understand what capability evidence the role may be testing, such as analysis, data management, product thinking, technical delivery, user focus, governance, or communication. Do not copy the framework language mechanically into the statement. The framework should guide evidence selection and gap analysis; the final wording should still be grounded in the candidate's own work objects and defensible examples.

## Not All Experience Has The Same Job

Each evidence source should be used for the claim it can honestly support.

For example:

- A fraud platform may support SQL, Python, modelling, monitoring, governance, risk readouts, and analytical delivery.
- A Power BI project may support dashboarding, metric explanation, data quality, and non-technical communication.
- Five Guys or Starbucks-style frontline work may support pressure, customer communication, procedure-following, queue/service flow, accuracy, and teamwork.
- A university society may support organising, communication, initiative, or collaboration.
- A self-directed portfolio project may support a missing domain-adjacent evidence need if the project has actually been completed.

Do not force every experience to do every job.

A part-time retail or hospitality role should not be exaggerated into a senior analytics claim. But it can be powerful evidence for behaviours, reliability, communication, customer focus, and operational discipline.

## Grounded Means Concrete

Grounded writing uses concrete nouns and real work objects.

Weak:

> I am passionate about using data to improve services and communicate insights clearly.

Stronger:

> I cleaned inconsistent referral fields, created validation checks, built a Power BI dashboard showing monthly demand and waiting-time trends, and wrote a short findings note explaining limitations for a non-technical reader.

Grounded evidence often names:

- datasets;
- reports;
- dashboards;
- validation checks;
- logs;
- handover notes;
- source tables;
- field definitions;
- stakeholder questions;
- findings notes;
- review packs;
- procedures followed;
- decisions supported.

Avoid polished adjectives when a concrete object can carry the proof.

## The Selection-Risk Memo

Think of the statement as a memo that lowers the panel's selection risk.

The panel is asking:

- Can this person actually do the job?
- Do they understand the setting?
- Are they honest about their boundaries?
- Are they strong enough to justify an interview?
- Will they need too much interpretation or rescue from us?
- Do they understand our behaviours and values?
- Can they communicate in the tone this work requires?

Each paragraph should reduce one concern.

Example concern and answer pattern:

- Concern: Can they do the technical core?
- Answer: Strongest direct-fit project evidence.

- Concern: Do they understand governance, ethics, or public accountability?
- Answer: Data quality, documentation, confidentiality, limitations, explainability, and careful-use evidence.

- Concern: They lack direct sector experience.
- Answer: Honest transfer from governed, multi-source, decision-relevant work, plus any small domain-specific portfolio slice if created.

- Concern: Why this role?
- Answer: Specific motivation tied to the employer's problem, not generic interest.

## Argument Architecture

Do not use a lazy template such as:

- introduction;
- body;
- conclusion.

Use an argument architecture.

The exact paragraph count depends on the word limit, but the movement should usually follow this order.

### 1. Role-Fit Hook

Purpose: show that the candidate understands the role's real shape.

This should not be:

> I am writing to apply...

It should place the candidate inside the employer's problem.

Example shape:

> This role stands out to me because it combines [real role need], [setting], and [type of contribution], which aligns closely with my recent work in [candidate evidence lane].

The opening should be warm but not ceremonial.

### 2. Strongest Direct Fit

Purpose: prove the candidate can do the core job.

This paragraph should carry the strongest Green evidence:

- technical tools;
- relevant projects;
- domain-adjacent work;
- analytical outputs;
- concrete results;
- direct match to essential criteria.

Cluster related criteria naturally. Do not list them mechanically.

Use the rhythm:

> capability cluster -> brief evidence -> role relevance.

### 3. Quality, Governance, Communication, Or Behavioural Fit

Purpose: prove the candidate understands the setting, standards, and risk.

For public-sector, CPS, NHS, or regulated work, this paragraph is often decisive.

It may cover:

- confidentiality;
- data protection;
- accuracy;
- documentation;
- limitations;
- auditability;
- equal treatment;
- integrity;
- Civil Service values;
- CPS/public-service seriousness;
- communication with non-technical users;
- decision support.

Behaviours and values should be embedded, not bolted on.

Do not write:

> I demonstrate integrity.

Write the situation that shows integrity.

### 4. Honest Transfer And Gaps

Purpose: handle adjacent fit without apology or invention.

If there is no direct NHS, CPS, finance, legal, or sector-specific background, say the transfer through evidence.

Example shape:

> While my recent work has not been within [sector] directly, it has required [governed handling / accuracy / evidence-based judgment / stakeholder communication], which I see as directly relevant to this post.

This is stronger than pretending.

### 5. Distinctiveness And Close

Purpose: leave the panel with the reason to shortlist.

Do not close with generic gratitude only.

Close by naming the candidate's distinctive working posture.

Examples:

- evidence-first;
- careful with uncertainty;
- practical and user-focused;
- governance-aware;
- able to connect data quality, analysis, and decision use;
- calm under pressure;
- clear in communication.

## Asymmetric Rhythm

The statement should not give equal space to every criterion.

Some criteria are decisive. Some are supporting. Some are boundary conditions.

Weight the answer by:

- importance to the role;
- strength of the candidate's evidence;
- likely panel concern;
- word limit.

It is normal for two middle paragraphs to carry most of the evidence while the opening and closing stay shorter.

Mechanical symmetry creates dead writing.

## STAR, But Compressed

STAR is useful, but supporting statements usually cannot afford full interview-style STAR for every point.

Use compressed STAR:

- Situation/context: one phrase.
- Task/problem: one phrase.
- Action: concrete work done.
- Result/relevance: output, effect, or why it matters to the role.

Example:

> In a self-directed health-data project, I cleaned inconsistent referral fields, created validation checks, built a Power BI dashboard showing monthly demand and waiting-time trends, and wrote a short findings note explaining data limitations for non-technical review.

That sentence gives context, action, output, and relevance without becoming a report.

## The Statement Is Not The Full Report

The supporting statement does not need:

- every formula;
- every SQL query;
- every chart;
- all methodology detail;
- all findings in depth.

It needs enough to show:

- what the problem or context was;
- what the candidate did;
- what tools or methods were used;
- who the output was for;
- what changed or what the output demonstrated;
- why it matches the person specification.

The portfolio, interview, notebook, dashboard, or report can hold the deeper detail.

## Claim-Bearing Slices

Where evidence is missing, do not invent a story.

Build a small, realistic slice of work that creates the evidence. Then write from that evidence.

This is the safest and most useful concept from the conversation.

> Build claim-bearing slices.

A claim-bearing slice is not a fake project. It is a small truthful piece of work strong enough to carry a specific statement claim.

Examples:

- one Power BI dashboard page;
- one SQL query that extracts and groups data;
- one Excel validation sheet;
- one data dictionary;
- one one-page findings note;
- one before/after data-cleaning example;
- one chart showing a trend or variation;
- one paragraph explaining limitations;
- one short presentation pack;
- one screenshot or PBIX export;
- one README explaining assumptions and caveats.

The slice does not need to be a complete product. It needs to be defensible.

## The Claim Decides The Slice

Do not start with:

> Let me build a huge project.

Start with:

> What claim do I need to support?

Example:

If the advert wants health-data dashboarding, the claim might be:

> I can take messy health-style data, clean it, validate it, analyse trends, and turn it into a clear dashboard and findings note.

The backing slice could be:

- public or synthetic health dataset;
- cleaned fields;
- validation checks;
- one Power BI page;
- one findings note;
- one limitations paragraph.

Then the statement can truthfully say:

> To strengthen my applied health analytics experience, I completed a self-directed project using public health data. I cleaned the dataset, created validation checks, built a Power BI dashboard, and wrote a short findings note explaining trends, limitations, and possible service questions.

That is not lying if the work exists.

## Backing Asset Rule

Every evidence paragraph should have a backing asset or backing situation.

| Statement claim | Backing evidence |
| --- | --- |
| I built a dashboard | Screenshot, PBIX, dashboard export, or workbook |
| I analysed trends | Chart, notebook, SQL, or Excel workbook |
| I improved data quality | Before/after example, validation rules, QA log |
| I communicated findings | One-page findings note, presentation, or handover note |
| I handled pressure | Real work situation from Five Guys, Starbucks, project work, or operations |
| I understand service context | Short explanation tying analysis to decisions |
| I supported governance | Limitation note, data dictionary, caveat log, excluded-record rule |

If the backing evidence does not exist and cannot be created honestly before applying, do not make the claim.

## The Ethical Line

Acceptable:

- using real work from an existing role in a smarter way;
- using academic projects as applied evidence;
- using part-time work for behaviours and values;
- creating a self-directed portfolio slice before applying;
- saying a report was written for a non-technical audience if it was;
- saying a project used public data if it did.

Not acceptable:

- claiming a stakeholder presentation that never happened;
- claiming NHS/CPS/legal/finance experience that does not exist;
- claiming formal approval, governance ownership, or policy responsibility without evidence;
- saying a portfolio project was workplace work;
- inventing results;
- inventing audiences;
- inventing seniority.

The rule:

> Only make claims that are backed by something you can show, explain, or defend.

## Believable Evidence Comes From Believable Contexts

Do not underuse real environments.

Part-time, operational, academic, portfolio, and project environments can all produce useful evidence if the claim fits the environment.

Believable:

- A barista notices recurring stock shortages on Mondays and Fridays and helps track them for shift planning.
- A Five Guys crew member discusses queue pressure, order accuracy, customer issues, payment handoffs, and procedure-following.
- A master's student completes a Power BI project using public health data.
- A data project produces validation checks, caveat notes, and dashboards.

Not believable:

- A barista claims to have led a company-wide revenue strategy for Starbucks.
- A student claims to have presented to NHS service leads without doing so.
- A portfolio build is framed as live enterprise ownership.

The environment should naturally support the situation.

## Word Count Posture

The application form controls the final length.

General working assumptions:

- For criteria-heavy supporting statements, 1,000 to 1,300 words can be appropriate if the form allows 1,500.
- For character-limited forms, the statement must be compressed around decisive criteria.
- For short answers, use one or two compact evidence examples, not a full essay.

Do not write a 400-word statement for a dense person specification unless the form forces it.

Do not fill 1,500 words if the evidence is thin.

The word count should serve the selection argument.

## Civil Service, CPS, And Values Posture

For Civil Service, CPS, and similar panel-scored statements, the writing must show evidence of behaviours and values without sounding like a behaviour dictionary.

Common scoring concerns include:

- integrity;
- impartiality;
- honesty;
- fairness;
- public-service motivation;
- sound judgment;
- evidence-based decision-making;
- communication;
- prioritisation;
- working with others;
- following procedures;
- managing sensitive information;
- learning and adapting.

The statement should not merely say:

> I follow the Civil Service Code.

It should show conduct that aligns with the code:

- checking facts before drawing conclusions;
- documenting limitations;
- escalating issues appropriately;
- treating people fairly under pressure;
- using evidence rather than assumption;
- respecting confidentiality;
- communicating clearly and proportionately.

For CPS-style applications, be especially careful with:

- judgment;
- accuracy;
- objectivity;
- evidence handling;
- public confidence;
- fairness;
- concise written communication.

## Drafting Questions

Before drafting, answer these six questions.

### 1. What Is The Employer Really Trying To Buy?

Not job title. Not keywords. The real need.

Example:

> They need someone who can build useful, governed, explainable analytics in a complex healthcare setting.

### 2. Which Criteria Are Decisive?

Pick the criteria that define shortlisting.

Usually there are three to five true decision points, even if the person specification has many lines.

### 3. Which Evidence Reduces Doubt On Those Criteria?

Choose the strongest evidence slices.

Do not dump all experience.

### 4. Where Is Fit Adjacent Rather Than Direct?

Name the boundary honestly and frame the transfer.

### 5. Why This Role Specifically?

Tie motivation to the employer's problem.

Avoid generic passion.

### 6. What Is The Low-Risk Argument For Appointing This Candidate?

The answer to this question is the statement's spine.

## Statement Planning Table

Use this before drafting.

| Panel need | Evidence source | Direct/adjacent/red | Backing asset | Statement role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Technical core | Project/work evidence | Green | Notebook/dashboard/report | Main proof |
| Governance or accuracy | Validation/caveat work | Green/Amber | QA log, limitation note | Trust proof |
| Communication | Handover/finding note | Green/Amber | One-page note/deck | Audience proof |
| Behaviour under pressure | Frontline/ops work | Amber | Real incident | Values proof |
| Missing domain evidence | New slice if built | Red until built | Portfolio asset | Gap bridge |

Do not draft until the table makes sense.

## Writing Rules

Use:

- specific role-fit opening;
- concrete evidence;
- compressed STAR;
- real work objects;
- honest gap framing;
- values embedded through situations;
- active verbs;
- clear relevance to the role;
- confident but bounded claims.

Avoid:

- "I am passionate about..." as the main argument;
- "I meet the essential criteria because...";
- long chronological life story;
- repeating the CV;
- overclaiming;
- fake stakeholder audiences;
- unexplained technical jargon;
- claiming direct sector experience from adjacent evidence;
- writing a technical report inside the statement.

## Audit Checklist

Before finalising, ask:

- Does the opening show I understand the role's real problem?
- Are the strongest criteria answered early?
- Does each paragraph reduce a panel concern?
- Are behaviours and values visible through evidence?
- Are direct and adjacent claims clearly separated?
- Have I avoided pretending direct experience I do not have?
- Does every claim have a backing asset or real situation?
- Is the evidence concrete enough to feel real?
- Is it concise enough for the word limit?
- Could I defend every line at interview?
- Does it sound like me, not a generic applicant?

## Short Version

The complete method is:

1. Read the advert as the marking scheme.
2. Find the hidden hiring problem.
3. Build the candidate profile.
4. Map evidence to criteria.
5. Classify direct, adjacent, and red claims.
6. Decide the selection argument.
7. Build claim-bearing slices for missing but creatable evidence.
8. Draft with asymmetric rhythm.
9. Embed behaviours and values through situations.
10. Audit every claim for truth, specificity, and interview defensibility.

The goal is not to sound impressive.

The goal is to make the panel think:

> This candidate understands what we need, has credible evidence for the important parts, is honest about the boundaries, and is worth shortlisting.

# Supporting Statement Posture - Conversation Record

## Read This First

This document is a formatted record of a conversation. It is not a standalone guideline document, a prompt template, or a rewritten framework.

Read the full conversation from start to finish before using it. Do not lift isolated phrases, rules, examples, or conclusions out of sequence. The value is in the movement of the conversation: the user's prompts, the assistant's responses, the corrections, the narrowing of the idea, and the final reasoning about grounded supporting statements and claim-bearing evidence.

Any agent using this document must treat the transcript itself as the approved source of truth. The preamble below is only here to orient the reader before the conversation begins.

## Preamble

The conversation begins with a deliberately unsuitable or stretch job advert so that the exercise is not about manufacturing a real application. The purpose is to study how a strong supporting statement is reasoned into existence for panel-scored applications such as NHS, Civil Service, CPS, and similar public-sector roles.

The flow matters. It moves from reading the job advert and person specification, to creating a believable fictional candidate profile, to mapping evidence against what a sift panel would score, to planning and drafting a grounded statement, and then to extracting the lessons. Later in the conversation, the key ethical point becomes clearer: the aim is not to invent experience, but to identify real evidence, use believable contexts properly, and where needed build small claim-bearing slices of work that can honestly support future statement claims.

The reader should pay attention to how the conversation distinguishes between:

- a generic polished statement and a grounded statement;
- a CV summary and a selection argument;
- direct evidence, adjacent evidence, and claims that should not be made;
- believable use of part-time, academic, portfolio, project, and work evidence;
- creating truthful evidence slices before applying versus pretending experience already happened;
- writing enough to support a claim without turning the statement into a full technical report.

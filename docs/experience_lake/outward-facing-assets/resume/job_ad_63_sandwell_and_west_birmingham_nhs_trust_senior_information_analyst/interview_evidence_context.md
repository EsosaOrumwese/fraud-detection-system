## What the Project Was About

The project simulated the information reporting workload for an acute information team supporting **Surgical Services** and **Women and Child Health**.

It had four linked reporting areas:

1. **Elective waiting-list and referral-to-treatment reporting**
2. **Contracting and activity reconciliation**
3. **Maternity and perinatal reporting**
4. **Data quality, assurance commentary and business-continuity documentation**

The first area, elective waiting-list reporting, was included because the person specification asks for specialist knowledge of waiting-list or contracting processes, including national guidance and reporting requirements. The NHS England RTT statistics page explains that consultant-led Referral to Treatment waiting times monitor the length of time from referral through to elective treatment, and that monthly RTT data has been published since 2007, including incomplete pathway data for patients still waiting at the end of the reporting period. ([NHS England][1])

The second area, contracting and activity reconciliation, was included because the advert names contracting activity submissions and says the post supports financial planning, demand and capacity planning, performance assurance and system changes affecting contract submissions. For Eric, this was a natural extension of South Western close-out reporting, where the difference between "field-completed" and "ready for close-out" mattered commercially and operationally.

The third area, maternity reporting, was included because the advert names maternity services data submissions, maternity data quality compliance and perinatal reporting guidance. NHS England Digital publishes official monthly maternity statistics from the Maternity Services Data Set, covering NHS-funded maternity services in England, including activity at booking appointment, during pregnancy, during and after birth, and pregnancy outcomes; the publication has monthly frequency and granularity down to providers, NHS trusts, hospital sites and other geographies. ([NHS England Digital][2])

The fourth area, data quality and assurance, was included because the person specification asks for proven experience validating figures on complex reports, assimilating ambiguous information across a wide range of data, and data quality processes and audits. The JD also shows the postholder would analyse data using SQL, SQL Server Reporting Services and Excel, present accurate information in appropriate formats, check data quality, prioritise requests, support clinical groups, maintain an electronic knowledge base, keep national returns up to date, and ensure critical deadlines are achieved when managers are absent.

## What Data Was Used

The project used a combination of **public NHS data**, **official NHS guidance pages**, and **synthetic patient-level records**. This distinction is important.

Eric did **not** use confidential patient data. He did **not** use internal Trust PAS data. He did **not** claim to have submitted official NHS returns. The public data gave him real national reporting structures and provider-level statistics. The synthetic data allowed him to practise patient-level validation, pathway logic and submission checks without handling any real patient records.

The main public sources were:

**NHS England RTT waiting-times data and guidance pages.** These provided the public reporting context for elective pathways, incomplete waiting lists, monthly reporting and the distinction between completed and incomplete pathways. The RTT page also links to Waiting List Minimum Data Set information and RTT clock rules and guidance, which made it useful for understanding how national waiting-list reporting depends on definitions and snapshot rules. ([NHS England][1])

**NHS England Digital Maternity Services Monthly Statistics.** These provided the maternity reporting context. The publication is official statistics, published monthly, using data submitted to the Maternity Services Data Set, and includes maternity activity from booking through pregnancy, birth and outcomes. ([NHS England Digital][2])

**NHS Data Model and Dictionary.** This was used as the reference point for understanding how NHS reporting depends on approved information standards, datasets, data elements, attributes, classes and business definitions. The dictionary describes itself as a reference point for approved Information Standards Notices and says datasets provide specifications for data collections and analyses, while data elements are the data items used within datasets. ([NHS Data Dictionary][3])

**Hospital Episode Statistics documentation.** This was used to understand acute activity data concepts, especially admissions, outpatient appointments, administrative fields, clinical fields, geographic fields, snapshots, data quality, data dictionaries and the fact that HES is sourced from Commissioning Data Sets submitted through the Secondary Uses Service. NHS England Digital describes HES as a curated data product containing details about admissions, outpatient appointments and historical accident and emergency attendances, and notes it is used for monitoring trends, service planning, benchmarking and policy development. ([NHS England Digital][4])

The synthetic datasets were generated by Eric to simulate the kinds of records an information team might need to validate. They were not presented as real NHS patient data. They included:

- a synthetic referral and waiting-list table;
- a synthetic outpatient attendance table;
- a synthetic admitted care activity table;
- a synthetic theatre or procedure activity table;
- a synthetic maternity booking table;
- a synthetic delivery and birth outcome table;
- a synthetic contract activity reconciliation table;
- a synthetic data-quality exceptions table.

The synthetic records used plausible field structures, such as provider code, specialty, treatment function, referral date, clock start date, decision-to-admit flag, attendance date, procedure date, admission method, discharge date, maternity booking date, delivery date, gestation band, birth outcome, commissioner code, activity month and submission status. The point was not to create fake NHS facts. The point was to practise reporting rules and validation logic on data that behaved like operational healthcare data.

## Technical Setup

The project was built locally using a Microsoft reporting stack because the job advert and person specification name SQL, SQL Server Reporting Services, SQL Server Integration Services, Power BI and Excel.

Eric set up a local SQL Server database with separate areas for raw staging, transformed reporting tables, validation outputs and publication-ready summary tables.

The database had tables such as:

- `stg_rtt_provider_month`
- `stg_maternity_month`
- `stg_synthetic_waiting_list`
- `stg_synthetic_maternity_activity`
- `dim_provider`
- `dim_month`
- `dim_specialty`
- `dim_metric`
- `fact_waiting_list_snapshot`
- `fact_contract_activity`
- `fact_maternity_activity`
- `fact_data_quality_exception`
- `report_board_summary`
- `report_waiting_list_exception`
- `report_maternity_quality`
- `report_contract_reconciliation`

SQL Server Integration Services was used to build repeatable extraction and load flows. The project did not rely on manual copying and pasting into spreadsheets. The packages loaded public extracts and synthetic files into staging tables, applied basic field standardisation, checked required fields, created derived reporting fields and loaded report-ready tables.

SQL was used for transformations and validation. Eric wrote queries to check duplicates, missing provider codes, invalid dates, records falling outside reporting months, waiting-time values that did not reconcile to date differences, maternity records with delivery dates before booking dates, missing outcome fields, and activity records that appeared in one source but not another.

SQL Server Reporting Services was used to build the report outputs. These included:

- a monthly waiting-list performance report;
- a contracting activity reconciliation report;
- a maternity data-quality report;
- a board-style assurance summary;
- an exception report for records requiring correction or confirmation.

Power BI was used for interactive reporting, with pages for waiting-list trend, maternity activity, data quality exceptions, contracting reconciliation and assurance commentary.

Excel was used as the validation pack. This was deliberate. Even in technical information teams, Excel remains important for checking, reconciliation, sign-off and communicating figures to users. The Excel pack included pivot summaries, variance checks, month-on-month comparison, exception lists, formula checks and charts.

## What the Project Produced

The project produced six main artefacts.

The first was a **SQL Server database** that separated raw staging data, cleaned data, reporting facts, dimensions, validation exceptions and final report tables. This showed that Eric could structure reporting work rather than leaving it as one flat spreadsheet.

The second was a set of **SQL Server Integration Services packages** that automated the loading and validation of the public and synthetic files. This mattered because the role mentions SQL Server Integration Services, report automation and scheduled publication. It also showed business-continuity thinking: another analyst should be able to rerun the package rather than manually rebuild the report.

The third was a **SQL query library**. The library included extraction queries, validation queries, reconciliation queries, exception queries and report-output queries. Each query had comments explaining the purpose, input table, output table and validation logic.

The fourth was a set of **SQL Server Reporting Services reports**. These were designed to look like operational information reports: summary at the top, exceptions underneath, then drill-down detail. They were not just tables. They included commentary fields, prompts for month and provider, and clear notes where figures needed caution.

The fifth was a **Power BI dashboard**. This had pages for elective waiting-list trends, maternity activity, data quality exceptions and contracting activity. It used visual cues, filters and drill-down pages, but it did not try to impress with unnecessary design. It was built around questions a divisional manager or performance lead would ask: what changed, where is the pressure, which figures are reliable, which records need correction, and what needs to be escalated?

The sixth was a **reporting assurance pack**. This was the most important artefact. It included:

- data dictionary;
- field definitions;
- source-to-output lineage;
- validation checklist;
- query library index;
- monthly runbook;
- known limitations;
- commentary template;
- business-continuity handover notes;
- sign-off checklist.

This was built because the role is not only technical. The person specification expects written skills, documenting processes and tasks to aid delegation, and the JD expects the postholder to maintain knowledge bases, guidance, example queries and explanatory notes.

## Key Findings

The first finding was that **definitions drive the report more than the dashboard design**. The public RTT data was useful, but the meaningful reporting question was not simply "how many people are waiting?" It was whether the figures referred to incomplete pathways, completed admitted pathways, completed non-admitted pathways, decision-to-admit cases, or another reporting cut. NHS England's RTT page explains the difference between completed and incomplete pathway data and the publication history of those measures. ([NHS England][1]) Eric's finding was that a senior information analyst must never let a dashboard label hide a definition.

The second finding was that **final and provisional figures must be handled differently**. The maternity statistics page clearly shows monthly releases with final and provisional periods, such as "Final February 2026, Provisional March 2026." ([NHS England Digital][2]) In the project, Eric separated final and provisional months in the report model so that users would not compare a provisional month against a final month without a note. That was important because a provisional dip or spike might reflect submission timing, not service change.

The third finding was that **data-quality exceptions should be published as part of the report process, not hidden in analyst notes**. In the synthetic waiting-list and maternity data, validation checks flagged records such as duplicate pathway IDs, missing specialty codes, missing provider codes, date sequences that did not make sense, maternity delivery dates before booking dates, missing outcome fields, and records appearing in operational activity but not in the submission-ready table. Eric designed the reports so that these exceptions appeared in a data-quality page and a separate exception report.

The fourth finding was that **contracting activity needs reconciliation between operational activity and submission-ready activity**. This mirrored Eric's South Western experience. At South Western, a job could be field-completed but not ready for close-out. In the NHS reporting project, an activity record could be operationally visible but not submission-ready because a required field was missing, an activity date was outside the reporting month, a commissioner code was absent, or a pathway status was unclear. The finding was that operational monitoring and contracting submission should be related, but not treated as identical.

The fifth finding was that **board commentary should explain the "why," not only the "what."** The Power BI dashboard could show trends and exceptions, but the board summary needed narrative commentary: what changed from last month, whether the change was likely operational or data-quality related, what was provisional, what required service confirmation, and which risks needed escalation. This matched the job advert's emphasis on assurance commentary for internal boards and external scrutiny.

The sixth finding was that **business continuity depends on documentation as much as technical skill**. Eric wrote the runbook so another analyst could refresh the input files, run the SQL Server Integration Services packages, review validation exceptions, open the SQL Server Reporting Services reports, refresh the Power BI dashboard, update the commentary, and identify records requiring service confirmation. This was important because the job advert names business continuity for statutory reporting during staff absence.

The seventh finding was that **a report can be technically correct but operationally unsafe if the audience is not told what the data can and cannot prove**. For example, a rising waiting-list count might reflect genuine pressure, a change in referral volume, a change in validation, a coding issue, or late submission. A maternity activity trend might be affected by final and provisional reporting timing. A contracting activity variance might be due to missing fields rather than real loss of activity. Eric built commentary templates to make those cautions visible.

## What Eric Learned

Eric learned that NHS information reporting is not just a technical task. It is a chain of accountability.

A national definition affects a local query. A local query affects a published figure. A published figure affects a board discussion. A board discussion affects operational action. If the definition is wrong, the wrong people may act on the wrong message. That was the central lesson.

He also learned that public data is not enough by itself. Public data can help with understanding national reporting structures, trends and definitions. But a senior information analyst in a Trust would also need internal PAS data, maternity system data, contracting data, service-manager knowledge, local validation rules, sign-off processes and guidance from senior information colleagues. So Eric's project did not claim to replace NHS experience. It demonstrated that he had learned the discipline and could talk credibly about the reporting process.

He also learned that tools must serve the reporting process. SQL Server Integration Services was not used because it looked impressive. It was used to make loading repeatable. SQL Server Reporting Services was not used as a badge. It was used to create paginated reports that could be filtered and published. Power BI was not used for decoration. It was used to show trends and exceptions. Excel was not used as a fallback. It was used as a validation and reconciliation tool. The SQL query library was not created for show. It made the report maintainable.

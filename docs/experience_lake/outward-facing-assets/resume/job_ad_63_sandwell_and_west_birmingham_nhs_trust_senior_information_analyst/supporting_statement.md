# Supporting Statement

I am applying for the Senior Information Analyst role because my experience is strongly aligned with the core of the post: producing accurate, validated information from complex datasets; developing and maintaining reporting outputs; explaining performance trends and anomalies; supporting data quality; and helping teams use information to make better operational decisions. I understand this role supports Surgical Services and Women and Child Health, including contracting activity, maternity data submissions, national targets, performance monitoring, assurance commentary and urgent executive or regulatory information requests. I would bring strong reporting discipline, advanced analytical tools, data-quality assurance and experience supporting others to produce accurate outputs under deadline pressure.

I meet the formal numeracy and qualification requirements through WASSCE Mathematics grade A, IGCSE Mathematics grade A, a First Class BEng Mechanical Engineering degree from Covenant University, and an MSc Data Science with Artificial Intelligence from the University of Exeter, completed with Distinction. My degrees required advanced numerical reasoning, statistics, modelling, data analysis, technical writing and interpretation of complex outputs. I am also willing to complete the required supervisory management qualification, as I see it as a natural extension of the task-coordination, trainee support and reporting-quality responsibilities I have already undertaken.

My strongest workplace evidence comes from my Data Analyst role at South Western Technologies & Oilfield Services Ltd, where I supported operational information reporting across service activity, equipment movement, QHSE, man-hours, close-out documentation and management information. The reporting environment was operationally demanding: reports were used to understand work status, delays, documentation readiness, equipment availability, data quality and follow-up actions. I worked with Oracle data extracts, SAS programs, Excel reporting workbooks, Access datasets, Power BI dashboards and SQL reporting logic to prepare recurring reports and exception views. This gave me direct experience of turning large, detailed operational records into management-ready information.

A typical reporting problem was that a job could be shown as completed in one source but still be missing a signed service report, equipment return confirmation, man-hour record or close-out status. If that was not picked up, the report could give a false view of readiness. I therefore separated raw extracts, cleaned datasets, exception views and final reporting outputs. I created reporting categories such as active, delayed, field-completed, awaiting documentation, awaiting equipment confirmation and ready for close-out. This is highly relevant to information reporting because it shows my ability to interpret ambiguous status information, choose the most appropriate reporting definition and present figures in a way that supports action rather than confusion.

I have strong experience with the reporting and analysis tools required for this post. In Excel, I have built structured workbooks using formulas, pivot tables, lookups, conditional formatting, validation checks, charts, percentages, variances, trends and management summaries. I have used macros and VBA to automate repeated workbook checks, formatting and report-preparation steps. In Access, I have used tables, queries and controlled local datasets where a spreadsheet alone was not robust enough for recurring reporting inputs. In Power BI, I have produced dashboards showing trends, exceptions, status views, data-quality measures and drill-down views. I have also used SQL to create reporting queries, joins, validation checks and curated outputs. Alongside workplace reporting, I have developed practical SQL Server Reporting Services and SQL Server Integration Services experience through self-directed reporting practice, using them to build report outputs and repeatable extraction, loading and transformation workflows. I am also competent with Word, text editors, email, internet tools and technical documentation.

Data validation is one of my strongest areas. At South Western, I validated complex reports by checking row counts, duplicate records, missing fields, inconsistent status logic, unclear service categories, failed joins, unexpected movements between reporting periods and differences between source extracts and final outputs. In QHSE reporting, I learned not to treat missing submissions as true zero values. A low recorded incident figure could reflect low risk, but it could also reflect incomplete reporting. I used rates such as observations per 1,000 man-hours, percentage changes, trend views, standard deviation, median and mean comparisons, Pareto breakdowns, variance review and completeness checks to ensure the figures were interpreted safely before they were used.

I am confident working with highly complex or ambiguous information across several data sources. In my Data Analyst role, Oracle records, SAS transformations, Access tables, Excel trackers, QHSE submissions, man-hour records, equipment movement notes and close-out files did not always agree. My role was to identify which source answered which question, reconcile differences where possible, and make uncertainty visible where clarification was still needed. This is the same discipline I would bring to NHS reporting: a figure should not be treated as submission-ready or board-ready until its source, definition, exclusion rules, timing, validation checks and commentary are clear.

I do not claim direct employment in an NHS Acute Information Department, but I understand why this post requires waiting-list, contracting and national reporting knowledge. My relevant experience is in operational reporting where definitions, source selection, validation checks, reporting periods, exclusions and commentary determined whether figures could be trusted. I have developed working knowledge of NHS waiting-list, contracting and maternity reporting principles, and would apply local Trust guidance carefully rather than assume that a familiar-looking metric means the same thing in an acute NHS context.

I would approach national guidance updates by identifying what has changed, mapping the impact on existing reports, updating SQL logic or reporting definitions, testing outputs, documenting the change and explaining the operational consequence to users. My strength is not just producing a report; it is making sure the report is accurate, explainable and fit for the decision it supports.

# Preliminary Responses - Job Ad 03 - InHealth Group

## Q1. Based on your experience on the platform, tell me about a time you produced monthly and ad hoc reports using Excel and SQL, and how they were used in supporting operational and regional teams.

One strong example came from a bounded reporting cycle covering February 2026 and March 2026, where the need was to give two audiences, operations and regional oversight, a dependable monthly view of programme performance and then answer the most obvious follow-up question without rebuilding the logic from scratch. The immediate situation was that the monthly reporting lane needed to do two things at once. First, it had to show the current position against the prior month in a way that was stable and trustworthy. Second, it had to support a realistic ad hoc follow-up request from the same cycle, because a monthly pack on its own was not enough if it could not explain where the pressure actually sat. So the monthly and ad hoc reports were related, but they were not the same task. The monthly report was the recurring reporting requirement. The ad hoc output was a follow-up cut driven by what the monthly pack showed.

I built that reporting cycle from a fixed KPI family of four headline measures: flow volume, case-open conversion, confirmed outcome quality, and the share of the £50+ band as the stable follow-up lens. SQL did the core heavy lifting. I kept the work inside filtered SQL scans over the two selected months rather than broad in-memory handling, and built one compact month-band aggregate that reduced the reporting base to eight rows, two months across four amount bands. That aggregate gave me a repeatable foundation for both the recurring monthly pack and the ad hoc follow-up output. Excel was then the practical reporting surface for shaping the monthly pack into something operational and regional users could actually work with: current-versus-prior comparisons, KPI movement, structured pack layout, and the final audience-facing view. In other words, SQL created the governed analytical base, and Excel made the output usable in reporting terms.

The goal of the monthly report was not just to state numbers. It was to show whether the lane was stable, deteriorating, or masking a more concentrated problem. The monthly pack showed that volume had risen materially from 73,652,566 in February 2026 to 81,360,532 in March 2026, but the overall case-open rate stayed flat at 9.63% and confirmed outcome quality also stayed effectively flat at 19.86% versus 19.87% in the prior month. That mattered because it ruled out an inaccurate “whole programme decline” story. Instead, the report showed that the right next question was more targeted: which segment still needed closer review?

That is where the ad hoc report came in. Because I had built the monthly reporting cycle from a stable KPI base, I was able to answer that follow-up without redefining the logic. The ad hoc output focused on the £50+ band, which remained the clearest pressure area in the March 2026 lane. That segment carried 10.20% of flow share, converted into case work at 10.82%, and returned weaker confirmed outcome quality at 18.11%. So the follow-up output was used to support a more precise operational and regional discussion: not whether the whole lane was failing, but whether one concentrated segment justified targeted review. That changed the nature of the conversation. Instead of broad escalation, the reporting supported a narrower and more operationally useful response.

What makes that example important is that the reporting was dependable in both recurring and ad hoc form. The same KPI meanings were kept stable between the two outputs. The cycle had supporting controls around it, including KPI definitions, usage notes, caveats, a run checklist, and rerun instructions, and the release posture passed 4 out of 4 checks. That meant the teams were not reacting to one-off extracts or hand-built numbers. They were working from a controlled reporting lane that could be rerun and defended.

So, in practical terms, the monthly report supported regular operational and regional review by showing the current position against the prior month, while the ad hoc report supported targeted follow-up on the one segment that still looked structurally weaker. It was a regular reporting event in the sense that the monthly cycle itself was recurring. The ad hoc element was triggered by that cycle, but it was not random; it came from the same governed base and answered the natural next question raised by the monthly pack. That is the kind of reporting work I would bring to this role: recurring outputs that are stable and decision-useful, and follow-up analysis that is fast, controlled, and rooted in the same trusted logic.

## Q2. Mention a time or scenario where you had to regularly maintain, validate, and reconcile patient-level datasets to ensure reporting accuracy. What did that situation look like? How did you regularly maintain it, validate it and reconcile it? What kind of datasets and amount did you work on in all this? How was this relevant to your reporting accuracy? What kind of reporting if I may ask? How did you quantify the accuracy of this reporting?

I would answer that carefully, because I have not worked on literal NHS patient-level datasets. The closest direct equivalent from my experience was a detailed case-level reporting dataset that had to be maintained, validated, and reconciled tightly enough that downstream monthly reporting stayed accurate and defensible.

The clearest example came from a March 2026 stewardship window where I was supporting a monthly reporting lane built off three linked source families: monthly flow records, rolled case-state data, and truth-label data. The reporting risk there was not missing data in the simple sense. It was grain mismatch. The flow surface was already one row per flow_id, but the raw case surface was event-grain, not reporting-grain. For the linked March subset, 7,835,199 flows connected to case activity, but those same linked flows sat behind 20,581,909 raw case-event rows, which meant an average of 2.63 raw case-event rows per linked flow. If I had joined the raw case events directly into the reporting dataset, I would have duplicated records and distorted the monthly output.

So the regular maintenance part was about controlling the dataset at the correct reporting grain. I maintained it as one row per flow_id where the monthly flow had converted into case-opened work, with one rolled case state and one truth state attached. That gave me a maintained detailed dataset of 7,835,199 rows, with 7,835,199 distinct flow_id and 7,835,199 distinct case_id. In practical terms, maintaining it meant not just rebuilding the table, but preserving the grain, the key fields, the field authority rules, and the conditions under which it was safe to use for reporting.

Validation was the next layer. I ran repeatable checks to confirm the dataset was still fit for reporting use. In that example, the validation pack passed 5 out of 5 checks: monthly flow IDs were unique, the truth surface was unique at flow_id, the maintained dataset stayed unique at flow_id, required fields were complete, and the maintained dataset contained only case-opened rows. That mattered because the reporting was only as trustworthy as the maintained dataset underneath it. If the dataset had duplication or field-completeness issues, the reporting would look precise while actually being wrong.

Reconciliation was where I proved that the maintained dataset was not just internally tidy, but externally accurate against the reporting lane it was supposed to support. I reconciled the protected summary back to the monthly reporting output across all four amount bands: less than 10, 10 to 25, 25 to 50, and 50 plus. The reconciliation matched exactly on 4 out of 4 bands for flow rows, case-opened rows, and truth rows. That was the strongest proof point in the whole exercise, because it showed that once the grain was controlled properly, the maintained dataset reproduced the downstream reporting exactly rather than approximately.

The reporting this protected was monthly operational reporting. Specifically, it protected the same monthly case-open and truth-quality readings that fed the recurring reporting lane. From the maintained dataset, I was able to preserve an overall case-open rate of 9.63 percent and overall truth quality of 19.86 percent, while also preserving the band-level pattern, including the weaker 50 plus segment. So this was directly relevant to reporting accuracy because the dataset stewardship work prevented a structurally unsafe join from inflating or distorting the very KPIs that the monthly pack depended on.

So if you ask how I quantified reporting accuracy, I would not answer that vaguely. I quantified it in three ways: first, structural integrity, because the maintained dataset held at 7,835,199 rows with one row per flow_id; second, validation integrity, because all 5 out of 5 repeatable checks passed; and third, reporting reconciliation, because the downstream reporting matched exactly across all 4 amount bands and preserved the protected monthly readings of 9.63 percent case-open rate and 19.86 percent truth quality. That is the closest truthful analogue from my experience. It is not a claim of direct patient-record stewardship in a healthcare trust. It is a real example of maintaining, validating, and reconciling a sensitive detailed reporting dataset at scale so that downstream monthly reporting remained accurate, controlled, and defensible.

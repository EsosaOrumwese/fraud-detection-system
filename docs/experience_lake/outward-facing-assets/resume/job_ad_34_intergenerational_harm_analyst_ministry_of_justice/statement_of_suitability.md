The requirements for this role match the way I have built my analytical evidence: working with complex multi-source data, testing whether linked records are trustworthy, and turning technical analysis into cautious decision support. My strongest evidence comes from a governed fraud-risk analytics platform, policy-facing COVID-19 government-response analysis, and network analysis. Across these examples, the common thread is Python-led quantitative analysis, linked-data thinking, quality assurance, and public-interest judgement.

In the fraud-risk analytics platform, the analytical problem was not one clean table. The data included operational events, decision outputs, entity histories, labels, case movement, controls, and reporting evidence. The practical task was to turn those separate records into reliable evidence for prioritisation, workload understanding, quality review, and bounded demand planning without losing lineage or creating misleading joins. I used Python and SQL-led workflows to inspect grain, check keys, validate relationships, and separate event-level, entity-level, label-level, and case-level logic. I treated linkage as an analytical method in its own right because a wrong join could make a decision look effective when the evidence actually belonged to a different record, period, or outcome.

That work produced decision-support evidence rather than raw model output alone. For example, the highest-risk band produced 2.29x baseline yield, which showed that relevant cases were more concentrated in the top band than in the wider population. Bounded demand planning achieved 7.31% test MAPE, which gave a practical view of forecast error and how cautiously the output should be used. The value of these measures was not the numbers by themselves; it was that they helped judge where review effort could be concentrated, what workload pressure might follow, and what confidence to place in the output. This is the discipline I would bring to linked analytical work: always connect the metric to the decision, the data quality, and the limitation.

Quality assurance was central to the work. I used schema checks, completeness review, reconciliation, source traceability, leakage controls, and documented assumptions to test whether data was suitable for analysis. When discrepancies appeared, I separated possible source-data issues from transformation errors, modelling errors, control logic, matching logic, and interpretation problems before drawing conclusions. That discipline matters in linked-data work because matching errors can create both false positives and false negatives: records may be connected when they should not be, or missed when a relationship exists but is weak, incomplete, or inconsistently recorded. My outputs were designed to support prioritisation and review, not to remove human judgement. I documented validation outcomes and handover notes so another reviewer could understand why a result was produced, what source evidence it depended on, and what caution should follow.

I also bring public-interest and policy-relevant analytical experience. In a COVID-19 policy and activity analysis, I combined 1,004 daily CSV files in Python into 2,396,549 observations and 12 variables across 220 countries and 1,004 days. I then integrated Facebook Business Activity Trends data with Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker policy data to examine activity patterns in policy context. Before interpreting results, I assessed coverage, missingness, duplicates, country/date validity, metric suitability, and limitations. Combining those sources required care over provenance, definitions, coverage gaps, and the risk of overstating what the data could support. I produced visual analysis linking unusual movements to containment policies, public-health events, and documented external evidence. This gave the work a policy frame: what changed, when it changed, which policy context surrounded the change, and what could be stated responsibly from the data.

A separate network-analysis project strengthened my understanding of hidden relationships and prevention logic. I applied game-theoretic network analysis to a surveillance-prioritisation problem, using network structure to reason about hidden relationships, risk coverage, and prioritisation choices. The important discipline was not to treat network metrics as self-explanatory. A central node, bridge, or high-risk cluster only matters when the method, assumption, and practical implication are explained clearly. This is relevant to work involving linked records because risk is not always visible from a single row of data. It can sit in relationships, repeated events, weak signals, and gaps in coverage.

My communication approach is to write and speak at the level needed by the audience. For technical readers, I explain linkage logic, model validation, leakage controls, assumptions, quality checks, and why a method is suitable. For non-technical readers, I translate the same evidence into what it means for prioritisation, workload, confidence, risk, or caution. For example, I would not leave 2.29x baseline yield as a model statistic; I would explain that the top band is more concentrated with relevant cases and may support prioritised review if the data quality checks hold. I would not present 7.31% MAPE as a technical result in isolation; I would explain what it says about likely planning error and how carefully the forecast should be used.

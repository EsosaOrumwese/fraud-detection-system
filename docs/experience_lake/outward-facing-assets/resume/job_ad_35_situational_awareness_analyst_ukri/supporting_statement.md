My strongest structured-data example is a COVID-19 policy and activity analysis. The question was how business activity patterns changed across countries during the pandemic, and how those changes could be interpreted alongside government response measures. I combined 1,004 daily CSV files in Python into 2,396,549 observations and 12 variables across 220 countries and 1,004 days. I then integrated Facebook Business Activity Trends data with Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker data so that activity movements could be interpreted in policy context rather than as isolated time series.

Before drawing conclusions, I checked coverage, missingness, duplicates, country/date validity, metric suitability, and source limitations. This was essential because an apparent change could have come from data coverage, invalid keys, or a source limitation rather than a real-world movement. The analysis therefore used statistical judgement and quality assurance before interpretation. The output explained what changed, when it changed, which policy context surrounded the change, and what could be stated responsibly from the evidence. This is the same discipline I would bring to structured research-landscape data: assess whether the dataset is fit for comparison, choose proportionate methods, and make limitations visible.

That work also demonstrates the synthesis required for situational awareness and horizon scanning. I used visual analysis to expose timing and pattern changes, then linked those movements to documented containment policies, public-health events, and known limitations in the data. I avoided overstating causality by separating four things: what the data showed, what the timing suggested, what external evidence supported, and what needed further investigation. That approach is useful when identifying emerging trends, weak signals, knowledge gaps, or opportunities across a wider system, because the value is not the chart itself; it is the concise judgement that helps someone understand what may matter next.

## Q2 - Core analytical skills and tool use

**Question**

Tell me of a time or a situation where you had to showcase intermediate knowledge and high skill in the use of Microsoft Excel, and also understanding of SQL where you used this to write queries and showcased an analytical mindset and ability to explain trends and themes in data. Also, on top of those two, what is your understanding with regards to the importance of data quality?

**Situation**
- A stronger example came from the March month-end reporting cycle, where I was responsible for producing the pack used to review whether the current selection and case-opening approach was still producing acceptable workload and outcome quality.
- The people using it did not need raw extracts. They needed a reliable view of whether the month had broadly held together or whether one part of the operation was beginning to create too much burden for too little return.

**Task**
- My task was to build the monthly reporting base in SQL, present the final output in Excel, identify the real trend in the month’s results, and make sure the reporting was accurate enough to support review and follow-up decisions.

**Action**
- I used SQL first to query, filter, join, and aggregate the relevant source tables into one monthly reporting base. That part mattered because the data volumes were large and the records were linked in a way that could easily distort reporting if the joins were wrong. So I built the base at the correct reporting grain, calculated the core measures there, and checked the structure before anything moved into the reporting pack.
- I then used Excel as the working and presentation layer. I structured the output into a monthly pack with current-versus-prior comparisons, KPI summaries, and clear segmentation so the people reading it could see what had changed, what had held steady, and where the real pressure sat. My Excel use there was not just about formulas. It was about making the reporting readable, comparable, and usable.
- The analytical part came in how I interpreted the movement. In that period, overall volume rose from `73,652,566` to `81,360,532`, while case-open rate held at `9.63%` and confirmed-outcome quality remained effectively flat at `19.86%` versus `19.87%`. So the overall position was broadly stable. The real issue sat in the `50+` segment, which carried a `10.82%` case-open rate against a `9.49%` peer average and `18.11%` outcome quality against a `20.09%` peer average. That told me the real theme was not a whole-operation decline, but a concentrated pressure point within one segment that was creating more burden for less return.
- Data quality sat underneath all of that. In the same period, I had to control the reporting grain carefully because the linked March subset contained `7,835,199` reporting records sitting behind `20,581,909` raw event rows. If I had taken the raw events straight into the reporting base, I would have duplicated records and distorted the pack. So I rolled the event activity to one row per reporting record, ran repeatable validation checks, and reconciled the final dataset exactly across all four reporting bands before treating it as ready for use.

**Result**
- The result was a monthly reporting pack and follow-up analysis that were accurate enough to use, clear enough to act on, and specific enough to direct attention to the right place.
- SQL gave me the controlled reporting base. Excel gave me the structure to communicate the result properly. The analytical mindset was in separating the real theme from the noise: broad stability overall, with one segment needing closer review. The data-quality work made the reporting trustworthy enough to support that conclusion.
- My view of data quality is straightforward: if the grain, completeness, consistency, or reconciliation are wrong, then the trend interpretation is wrong, and the reporting stops being useful no matter how polished it looks. That is why I treat data quality as part of the analytical job itself, not as a final tidy-up step.

**Question**

Another needed to know whether confirmed outcomes were still holding up. Another needed enough detail to decide whether any specific segment needed corrective review before the next cycle. So the reporting had to do more than look polished. It had to help different people answer different operational questions from the same set of numbers.

**Task**
- My task was to take the monthly performance data and turn it into an accurate review pack using tables, charts, scorecard-style summaries, and dashboard-style views, so that the people reviewing the month could see whether the operation was broadly stable or whether one part of it was creating disproportionate burden for too little return.

**Action**
- I built the pack around a small number of measures that actually mattered to that review: overall reporting volume, case-open rate, confirmed-outcome quality, and comparative segment performance. Tables carried the exact figures and current-versus-prior comparisons. Charts and graphs made movement easier to see quickly. Scorecard-style summaries highlighted the indicators that needed attention. The wider dashboard-style layout let people move from the whole position to segment-level detail without getting lost.
- In that review, overall volume increased from `73,652,566` to `81,360,532`, while case-open rate held at `9.63%` and confirmed-outcome quality stayed effectively flat at `19.86%` versus `19.87%`. That meant the headline position was broadly stable. The important issue sat in the `50+` segment, where case-open rate rose to `10.82%` against a `9.49%` peer average while outcome quality sat at `18.11%` against a `20.09%` peer average. So the real story was not that the whole operation had weakened. It was that one segment was creating more workload for weaker return.
- One challenge was that the audience needed different depths from the same pack. Some wanted the headline quickly. Some wanted exact supporting numbers. Some wanted to know whether the movement was serious enough to justify intervention before the next cycle. I handled that by layering the output properly and by changing my explanation depending on the question being asked, while keeping the conclusion itself consistent.
- The other challenge was accuracy under complexity. The audience only saw the final pack, but the value of the pack depended on the reporting base being reconciled first. So I made sure the reporting base was controlled at the right grain, validated before presentation, and only then shaped into the charts, tables, and summaries they were using.

**Result**
- The result was that the pack could be used confidently by everyone in that review without creating confusion about what the month had actually shown.
- The headline readers could see that overall performance had broadly held. The people who needed precision had the supporting tables. And the people deciding what to review next could see that the real issue was concentrated in one segment rather than across the whole operation.
- That is the strongest example I have of presenting information accurately to varied audiences. It was not just a case of showing charts and tables. It was a case of making sure the right people could use the same information for different decisions without being misled by it.

## Q4 - Independent delivery, challenge, problem solving, and stakeholder handling

**Question**

Tell me of a time where you had to operate under pressure, where you had to challenge and manage user expectations, demonstrate excellent problem-solving skills, exercise your own initiative, work independently to achieve objectives and deadlines, showcase attention to detail, and still communicate well while handling the task under pressure.

**Situation**
- A stronger example came during the same March reporting window, when I was finalising the dataset behind the monthly performance pack that was due for review.
- The pack was supposed to support a live discussion about workload and outcome quality. While checking the data behind it, I realised the case-linked base was sitting at raw event grain rather than reporting grain. That meant if I pushed the pack through unchanged, the people reviewing it would be using inflated numbers to make decisions about the next cycle.

**Task**
- My task was to diagnose the issue quickly, rebuild the reporting base properly, protect the deadline as far as possible, and make it clear that the provisional version should not be used just because it looked plausible.

**Action**
- I worked through the issue independently because the immediate need was diagnosis, not discussion. I checked the reporting grain and found that the March linked subset contained `7,835,199` reporting records but sat behind `20,581,909` raw event rows. That told me straight away that the raw event data was not safe for direct reporting use.
- The problem-solving part was recognising what that meant in practical terms. If I had joined those raw event rows straight into the reporting base, the monthly pack would have overstated activity and created a false view of workload and performance. So I rebuilt the base at one row per reporting record, rolled the event activity to the correct level, and then reran the checks against the reporting totals.
- This is where challenge and expectation management came in. Under deadline pressure, it is easy for people to want the nearest available output, especially if the issue is not visible in the final chart. I had to hold the line that the original route was not safe enough to use and that a short delay for correction was better than presenting a pack that looked clean but was analytically wrong.
- I then explained the issue in simple terms: the first version would have counted the same reporting record multiple times because the source sat at the wrong level of detail. The rebuilt version preserved one row per reporting record and could therefore be defended. That kept the conversation on the operational risk of using the wrong pack, rather than on technical wording.

**Result**
- The result was that the reporting base was corrected before use and the monthly pack could be trusted. The rebuilt dataset stayed at `7,835,199` rows with one row per reporting record, passed `5/5` repeatable validation checks, and reconciled exactly across all four reporting bands.
- More importantly, the review itself was protected. The people using the pack were able to work from an accurate view rather than a distorted one, and the pressure of the deadline did not turn into pressure to lower the standard.
- That is the clearest example I have of how I operate under pressure: I work independently, diagnose the issue properly, challenge the unsafe route when necessary, manage expectations around what is genuinely fit to use, and keep the communication clear enough for people to understand why the correction matters.

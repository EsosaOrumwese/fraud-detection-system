## Q2 - Core analytical skills and tool use

**Question**

Tell me of a time or a situation where you had to showcase intermediate knowledge and high skill in the use of Microsoft Excel, and also understanding of SQL where you used this to write queries and showcased an analytical mindset and ability to explain trends and themes in data. Also, on top of those two, what is your understanding with regards to the importance of data quality?

**Situation**
- A strong example came from a monthly performance-reporting cycle in `Mar 2026`, where I was responsible for preparing a reporting pack used to review operational performance and identify where follow-up action was actually needed.
- The challenge was not just getting the numbers out. It was making sure the reporting was accurate, reconcilable, and clear enough for managers to distinguish between broad stability across the operation and a specific pressure point that was creating unnecessary workload.

**Task**
- My task was to build the monthly reporting base in SQL, present the stakeholder-facing output in Excel, identify the real trends and themes in the results, and make sure the final reporting was accurate enough to support operational review and follow-up decisions.

**Action**
- I used SQL first to query, filter, join, and aggregate the relevant source tables into one monthly reporting base. That part mattered because the data volumes were large and the records were linked in a way that could easily distort reporting if the joins were wrong. So I built the base at the correct reporting grain, calculated the core measures there, and checked the structure before anything moved into the reporting pack.
- I then used Excel as the working and presentation layer. I structured the output into a monthly pack with current-versus-prior comparisons, KPI summaries, and clear segmentation so the people reading it could see what had changed, what had held steady, and where the real pressure sat. My Excel use there was not just about formulas. It was about making the reporting readable, comparable, and usable.
- The analytical part came in how I interpreted the movement. In that period, overall volume rose from `73,652,566` to `81,360,532`, while case-open rate held at `9.63%` and confirmed-outcome quality remained effectively flat at `19.86%` versus `19.87%`. So the overall position was broadly stable. The real issue sat in the `50+` segment, which carried a `10.82%` case-open rate against a `9.49%` peer average and `18.11%` outcome quality against a `20.09%` peer average. That told me the real theme was not a whole-operation decline, but a concentrated pressure point within one segment that was creating more burden for less return.
- Data quality sat underneath all of that. In the same period, I had to control the reporting grain carefully because the linked March subset contained `7,835,199` reporting records sitting behind `20,581,909` raw event rows. If I had taken the raw events straight into the reporting base, I would have duplicated records and distorted the pack. So I rolled the event activity to one row per reporting record, ran repeatable validation checks, and reconciled the final dataset exactly across all four reporting bands before treating it as ready for use.

**Result**
- The result was a monthly reporting pack and follow-up analysis that were accurate enough to use, clear enough to act on, and specific enough to direct attention to the right place.
- SQL gave me the controlled reporting base. Excel gave me the structure to communicate the result properly. The analytical mindset was in separating the real theme from the noise: broad stability overall, with one segment needing closer review. And the data-quality work made the reporting trustworthy enough to support that conclusion.
- My view of data quality is straightforward: if the grain, completeness, consistency, or reconciliation are wrong, then the trend interpretation is wrong, and the reporting stops being useful no matter how polished it looks. That is why I treat data quality as part of the analytical job itself, not as a final tidy-up step.

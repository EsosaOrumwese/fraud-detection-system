## Evidence Group 1 - Strategic and Portfolio Understanding

Criteria attacked:
- Demonstrate ability to grasp the big picture.
- Understand critical drivers of business strategy.
- Understand the project delivery lifecycle.
- Understand key differences between Agile and Waterfall methodologies.

Raw response:

A strong example comes from the governed fraud risk intelligence platform, where the issue was not simply whether individual analytical outputs worked in isolation. The bigger question was whether the reporting evidence could support reliable decisions across a delivery chain: raw data arriving from multiple sources, transformations creating reporting surfaces, model outputs changing prioritisation, case flows creating operational workload, and control checks determining whether outputs could be trusted. If any part of that chain was weak, a technically correct metric could still create the wrong delivery decision.

I approached this by treating the platform as a delivery system rather than a collection of separate analyses. I mapped the work around decision needs: what evidence showed current performance, what showed delivery pressure, what showed control weakness, what needed follow-up, and what could be treated as resolved. This is where my understanding of delivery lifecycle matters. Some work followed an Agile pattern because the analysis evolved through discovery, testing, feedback, and refinement. Other work needed more Waterfall-style discipline because validation, documentation, and control gates had to be completed before outputs could be treated as reliable.

The result was a clearer single version of truth across operational, decision, control, and reporting evidence. Instead of only producing analysis, I could show which outputs were ready for use, which carried risk, which assumptions mattered, and where attention should go first. That maps directly to portfolio work because a PMO also needs to turn many moving parts into a coherent picture of delivery position, risk, value, and next action.

This evidence also shows the behaviour behind Seeing the Big Picture. I did not treat data quality, reporting, risk, and documentation as separate admin tasks. I connected them to decision quality, delivery confidence, service reliability, and the ability to prioritise limited attention.

## Evidence Group 2 - Analytical and Reporting Capability

Criteria attacked:
- Possess strong analytical skills.
- Use data to identify trends.
- Formulate sound conclusions from data.
- Produce insights that support senior-leader decision making.

Raw response:

A strong analytical example came from building reporting datasets and indicators across a large fraud decisioning environment. The data did not arrive as one clean table. It included operational events, decision outputs, entity histories, labels, case movement, control evidence, and reporting tables. The analytical problem was to make sense of these sources without losing lineage, confusing denominators, or presenting metrics that looked precise but were not decision-useful.

I used SQL, Python, and Excel to build reusable reporting datasets, validate joins, check completeness, reconcile outputs, and produce indicators for workload exposure, case flow, data integrity, model yield, and demand pressure. I investigated discrepancies by separating source-data issues from transformation, modelling, control, and interpretation issues. This mattered because the right conclusion depended on knowing whether a movement was a real operational signal or a data-quality artefact.

The outputs supported prioritisation and delivery-risk thinking. Ranked-risk analysis showed 2.29x baseline yield in the highest-risk band, meaning the scoring evidence could direct attention to areas with materially higher value. Bounded demand planning achieved 7.31% test MAPE, which gave a practical way to anticipate workload pressure rather than reacting after the fact. These metrics were not used as decoration; they helped answer where attention should go, what capacity pressure to expect, and what trade-offs needed explaining.

The portfolio relevance is that the same analytical discipline is needed when interpreting delivery data across projects and programmes. A useful portfolio analyst does not only report numbers. They test whether the evidence is reliable, identify trends and exceptions, explain what the trend means, and turn it into a conclusion decision makers can act on.

## Evidence Group 3 - Collaboration, Communication, and Stakeholder-Useful Outputs

Criteria attacked:
- Exhibit a strong team ethic.
- Collaborate and engage with impact.
- Display excellent communication skills.
- Simplify complex information for diverse stakeholders.

Raw response:

My strongest evidence here is the way I turned complex technical evidence into practical summaries that could be understood outside the technical build context. The fraud platform involved model outputs, data lineage, validation checks, leakage prevention, reconciliation, case-flow logic, and control boundaries. If these were communicated only in technical language, the key message would be lost: what risk exists, why it matters, what evidence supports the conclusion, and what action remains open.

I wrote evidence notes and reporting summaries around practical questions: what changed, what is reliable, what is not yet reliable, what assumption affects interpretation, what action is still open, and what decision the evidence supports. I avoided treating a dashboard or metric as self-explanatory. For example, I translated model-yield and demand-pressure findings into priority, workload, service reliability, and next-action implications.

I can also evidence this through operational work outside the platform. In field engineering, clear handovers and escalation mattered because technical readings and site conditions affected safe delivery. In live service work, communication, pace, and team support affect service quality every shift. These experiences reinforce the same working style: share useful information early, keep people aligned under pressure, and communicate in a way that helps others act.

This is the truth-safe way to frame stakeholder impact for this application. I am not claiming formal PMO stakeholder ownership. I am showing that my outputs are designed around user and decision needs, and that I can simplify technical or operational evidence into clear implications for people who need to act on it.

## Evidence Group 4 - Process Improvement, Productivity, and Governance

Criteria attacked:
- Drive simplification of processes.
- Leverage tools such as AI to enhance productivity and quality.
- Technical skill: Governance.

Raw response:

A strong example comes from improving how analytical risks, blockers, validation failures, and documentation were controlled. At the start, the danger was that issues could be found and fixed locally but not leave enough evidence to show what changed, why it mattered, what dependency it affected, or whether it was genuinely resolved. That would weaken governance because the work would rely too much on memory and individual judgement.

I used RAID-style discipline to organise blockers, control gaps, validation failures, dependencies, decision points, action status, and resolution evidence. I paired this with reusable validation checks, consistent definitions, documented assumptions, known limitations, and handover notes. This simplified repeat work because checks did not need to be reinvented each time and outputs could be reviewed against a consistent evidence trail.

I also used automation and controlled AI-assisted support where it improved productivity without replacing judgement. The important point is that AI-assisted drafting or coding support was reviewed critically, tested against the actual evidence, and kept within controlled workflows. For this role, that matters because the advert does not ask for novelty for its own sake; it asks for tools that improve productivity and quality in a newly forming PMO.

The result was stronger governance and a more repeatable way of working. Reporting evidence became easier to review, assumptions were easier to challenge, and issues were less likely to be marked complete without clear resolution evidence. That maps directly to PMO governance because portfolio reporting needs consistency, traceability, challenge, and disciplined evidence before it can support decisions.

# Supporting Statement - Betsi Cadwaladr University Health Board - Specialist Information Analyst

I am applying for the Specialist Information Analyst role because my experience combines operational information analysis, statistical interpretation, dashboard development, data-quality assurance and clear communication with decision-makers. I am particularly drawn to the role's focus on mental health data, routine and ad-hoc reporting, stakeholder support and preparation for a new Electronic Health Record, because this matches the way I have worked: understanding operational processes, tracing how source records become management information, checking data quality, and making reporting outputs clear enough for colleagues to act on.

I meet the education and statistical knowledge requirements through a First Class BEng Mechanical Engineering degree from Covenant University and an MSc Data Science with Artificial Intelligence from the University of Exeter, completed with Distinction. My postgraduate study covered statistics, data preprocessing, Python, modelling, visualisation, evaluation and research delivery. In my MSc research project, I worked with high-frequency smartphone sensor data, prepared model-ready windows, evaluated outputs using accuracy, precision, recall and F1-score, and documented limitations around sampling, class balance and generalisability. More importantly for this role, I have applied statistical thinking in operational reporting by using denominators, standard deviation, trend analysis, outlier checks, completeness checks and variation review to avoid misleading conclusions. In postgraduate and project work, I also applied confidence intervals and ANOVA to compare differences between groups, and used statistical process control to judge whether movement over time reflected normal variation or a change that needed investigation.

My strongest workplace evidence comes from my Data Analyst role at South Western Technologies & Oilfield Services Ltd, where I supported reporting across service activity, equipment movement, QHSE, man-hours, close-out documentation and management information. The work involved Oracle data extracts as structured operational sources, SQL and SAS for querying and repeatable analytical processing, Excel and Access for reporting inputs and checks, and Power BI for presenting trends, status views and exceptions. Where SQL was required, I used it to query and join operational records, test unmatched items and prepare extracts for reporting. A typical reporting problem was that a job could be shown as completed in one source but still be awaiting a signed service report, equipment return confirmation, man-hour record or close-out status. I used these tools to prepare recurring reporting outputs showing what was active, delayed, field-completed, awaiting documentation, awaiting equipment confirmation or ready for close-out.

This experience is directly relevant to the post because it required me to translate abstract operational data into information decision-makers could understand and use. Managers did not need a raw extract; they needed to know which jobs were ready, which were blocked, what was causing delay and who needed to act. I therefore built exception views and concise summaries rather than overwhelming colleagues with every raw field. One useful improvement was separating "field-completed" from "ready for close-out," because using one broad "completed" category hid important follow-up work. That same discipline applies to mental health reporting: the value of information is not the amount of data extracted, but whether operational leads, clinicians and senior managers can trust the definitions, see the issue clearly and use the output to improve services.

Data quality is central to how I work. At South Western, I checked row counts, duplicate records, missing fields, inconsistent statuses, failed joins, unclear service categories and unexpected movements between reporting periods. In QHSE reporting, I learned not to treat missing submissions as true zero values. A service line with low recorded incidents might reflect low risk, but it might also reflect incomplete reporting. I used rates such as observations per 1,000 man-hours, trend views, standard deviation, median and mean comparisons, Pareto breakdowns and completeness checks to improve interpretation. I would bring the same caution to Health Board reporting by challenging figures carefully and constructively where data appears incomplete, inconsistent or misleading.

I have strong experience with the Microsoft and analytical tools named in the person specification. In Excel, I have built structured workbooks, pivot tables, lookups, formulas, conditional formatting, validation checks, exception lists and management-ready summaries. In Access, I have used tables, queries and controlled local data structures where a spreadsheet alone was not robust enough for recurring reporting inputs. In Power BI, I have produced dashboards showing trends, exceptions, status views and data-quality measures. I have also used SQL, Python, SAS and Oracle across analytical work, including querying, joining, transforming, testing and preparing data. These tools were not used as isolated technical exercises; they supported repeatable reporting, quality assurance and clearer decision-making.

I recognise that this role requires familiarity with NHS information standards, National Data Dictionary items, NHS codes, National Minimum Datasets, Data Protection and Freedom of Information. I would not claim employment-based Health Board systems experience, but I understand the reporting discipline behind those requirements: definitions, codes, inclusion rules, exclusions, mappings, version control and data-quality checks determine whether a report can be trusted. I have applied that discipline in operational reporting and platform work by defining reporting categories, documenting fields, validating source-to-output lineage and checking whether changes in data flow reflected real operational movement or a recording issue. I also understand that the new EHR implementation creates a reporting risk as well as an opportunity. Reporting continuity will depend on controlling definitions, mappings, validation rules, historical comparability and dashboard measures so that system change is not mistaken for service change.

I understand the importance of information governance in this post. My working understanding is that data must be used lawfully, fairly, securely and proportionately, with clear purpose, minimum necessary access, accurate definitions, appropriate retention and documented accountability. For information requests, I would check definitions, date ranges, exclusions, validation evidence, small-number disclosure risk and whether figures should be aggregated before treating them as ready to share. In my platform work, I separated raw events from curated analytical outputs, restricted access to sensitive layers, avoided hard-coded credentials, documented lineage and made validation failures visible. For Health Board reporting, I would follow local policies carefully, seek guidance when needed, and treat patient, staff, financial and corporate information with the confidentiality and caution required.

Communication is one of my strongest transferable areas. At South Western, I supported weekly discussions with operations, equipment, QHSE, admin, commercial colleagues and managers. Different teams used the same terms differently, so I translated data issues into practical questions: whether a service report had been received, whether equipment had returned, whether a delay related to client approval, logistics, documentation or internal follow-up. I avoided blaming language and used feedback from colleagues closest to the work to improve the report. This helped discussions move from general disagreement about status to specific actions: chase the report, confirm equipment return, clarify the delay reason or leave the job on the exception list. Through my MSc, I also learned to explain technical modelling decisions, evaluation metrics and limitations clearly, combining numerical findings with narrative and context.

I can manage competing priorities without losing accuracy. At South Western, recurring reporting work often sat alongside operational questions from different teams, so I treated time-sensitive exceptions and data-quality blockers as priorities because they affected whether managers could rely on the weekly picture. Before sharing figures, I checked key fields, reconciled unusual changes and made clear where data was incomplete or required follow-up. This approach helped me work under pressure while protecting the accuracy of the report. I have also delivered a demanding MSc research project, built a deployed AWS data platform over a sustained period, and supported NYSC and industrial training students by walking them through reporting workbooks, Excel checks, operational definitions and exception interpretation.

I would bring to Betsi Cadwaladr University Health Board a strong combination of analytical depth, reporting discipline and people-focused communication. I am used to learning operational systems by tracing how fields, statuses, definitions, source records and validation checks affect final reporting. I would apply that same discipline to the Health Board's systems, mental health reporting flows, local definitions and national reporting requirements, with the aim of producing accurate, meaningful information that supports operational delivery, service improvement and strategic decision-making.

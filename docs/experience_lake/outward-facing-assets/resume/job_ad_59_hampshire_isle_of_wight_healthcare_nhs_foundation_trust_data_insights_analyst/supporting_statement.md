# Supporting Statement

I am applying for the Data & Insights Analyst role because my background matches the core requirements of the post: information handling, data analysis, statistical interpretation, business intelligence reporting, process improvement and clear communication with non-technical users. I hold an MSc Data Science with Artificial Intelligence with Distinction and a First Class BEng Mechanical Engineering degree, giving me strong numeracy, computer literacy and data literacy. I also have practical Data Analyst experience in an operational environment, where my work involved turning incomplete and scattered records into reliable management information. I understand that in an NHS setting, data is not abstract: it supports service managers, clinicians, patients, planning decisions and service improvement, so accuracy, context and clear explanation matter.

In my Data Analyst role at South Western Technologies & Oilfield Services Ltd, I supported operational reporting for oilfield service activity. The data related to client jobs, service lines, field locations, equipment, crews, mobilisation, completion dates, man-hours, QHSE records and close-out documentation. One recurring task was a weekly operations and close-out report. A job could be marked “completed” in one tracker but still be missing a signed service report, equipment return confirmation, man-hour record or close-out status. I used structured Excel workbooks, lookup formulas, pivot tables, validation checks and exception lists to separate field-completed jobs from jobs genuinely ready for close-out. This gave managers a clearer view of what was active, delayed, blocked, completed or requiring follow-up.

That experience directly supports the role’s requirement to provide analytical support for operational and strategic needs, improve processes and produce information colleagues can use. I learned that data quality is not just a technical issue; it is linked to real processes and decisions. Rather than simply reporting totals, I checked whether the data reflected the operational reality, clarified missing or conflicting records with colleagues, and made uncertainty visible. I also communicated findings in practical, non-blaming language, for example clarifying whether a job was awaiting documentation, equipment confirmation, client approval or internal follow-up. This is the same discipline I would bring to NHS reporting: understanding definitions, checking completeness, validating outputs and avoiding misleading conclusions.

I have also used statistical techniques in practical reporting. In QHSE and operational activity reporting, raw counts could be misleading. A service line with more observation cards was not automatically higher risk if it had more field activity or man-hours; a low count could also mean incomplete reporting. I used rates and denominators such as observations per 1,000 man-hours, descriptive statistics, trend views, median versus mean comparisons, standard deviation, outlier checks, Pareto-style category breakdowns and data-completeness checks. This helped separate high activity from high risk, true zero values from missing submissions, and normal variation from issues needing follow-up. I would bring the same caution to healthcare reporting, particularly where conclusions depend on sample size, variation, definitions and completeness.

I can evidence business intelligence, dashboarding, data flows, automation and AI implementation through my independent AWS-hosted fraud and transaction decisioning platform and wider analytics work. I have used Power BI and Excel-based dashboards to present trends, exceptions, status views and data-quality issues, with checks back to source records so users could understand both the headline figure and the underlying data. In the fraud platform, I built a production-shaped system covering event ingestion, validation, feature creation, rule/model decisioning, case creation, monitoring dashboards and offline evaluation. The dashboard layer showed event volumes, flagged cases, case status, reason codes, validation failures, rule triggers, score distributions, false positives, false negatives and threshold effects. This gave me practical experience of automated data flows, quality checks, decision-support reporting and analytical monitoring.

The platform also strengthened my understanding of AI in analytics delivery. I learned that implementing AI is not just training a model; it requires clear data inputs, validation, explainability, monitoring, threshold selection, error analysis and review workflows. A high accuracy score can hide poor minority-class performance; a drop in flagged cases can mean lower risk or a broken data flow; and an increase in review cases can reflect genuine risk or an overly sensitive threshold. I used classification metrics, precision/recall trade-offs, threshold analysis, distribution checks, trend monitoring and drift awareness to avoid shallow conclusions. This aligns with the desirable AI criterion and with the role’s need for advanced statistical and modelling knowledge, while keeping outputs understandable enough to support action.

My MSc and thesis provide formal evidence of advanced statistical and modelling knowledge. Across the programme I worked with Python, data preprocessing, classification, regression, machine learning, optimisation, feature selection, model evaluation and visualisation. My thesis, “Multi-Task Learning for Driver Identification and Transport Mode Classification,” used smartphone accelerometer, gyroscope and rotation-vector data to classify transport mode and identify drivers. I downsampled high-frequency sensor data, segmented journeys, created sliding-window samples, built deep learning models and evaluated results using accuracy, precision, recall and F1-score. The project strengthened my understanding of class imbalance, sampling choices, model limitations, reproducibility and responsible interpretation. This is relevant because the same discipline is needed when judging whether a model, metric or dashboard output is reliable enough to inform operational decisions.

I have also worked with public datasets in a way that supports the population and planning aspects of this role. In a public-data project, I combined 1,004 daily Facebook Business Activity Trends files into a 2.4 million-row dataset and analysed business activity across selected countries and sectors during COVID-19 alongside Oxford Government Response Tracker policy data. I used Python and pandas to combine files, check coverage, select the more stable activity-quantile metric, visualise time-series trends and interpret patterns against policy context without overstating causation. While this was not healthcare data, it gave me relevant experience in comparing groups over time, choosing appropriate measures, checking data coverage, interpreting trends in context and communicating limitations carefully — the same habits needed when supporting planning, population insight and service-improvement questions.

I have developed strong communication and people skills across both technical and frontline settings. At South Western, I explained reporting exceptions to operations, equipment, QHSE and admin colleagues in practical language. In my current Crew Member role at Five Guys, I work under pressure in a frontline service environment where accuracy, respectful communication, teamwork and clear handovers affect the customer experience. A busy shift can depend on clear status information: which orders are ready, which are waiting on fries or drinks, and what needs checking before handover. That has reinforced a principle I would bring to NHS analytics: reliable information helps frontline services act safely and confidently. As an early committee member of the University of Exeter Swing Dance Society, I also helped create beginner-friendly activity, welcomed new members and supported inclusive participation.

I recognise that I would need to develop direct NHS systems experience, FOI processes, NHS contract reporting, population-health analytics in a healthcare setting, NHS Making Data Count/SPC methods and formal PRINCE2 methodology. I would approach these honestly by learning the Trust’s systems, definitions, governance requirements and reporting procedures carefully, including confidentiality, information security, data opt-out, suppression/anonymisation rules and local quality assurance processes. What sets me apart is that I can combine practical operational reporting with advanced data science training, Power BI and Excel dashboard work, AI-enabled automation, public-data analysis and frontline service awareness. I can offer the Trust someone who understands both the technical discipline behind reliable insight and the practical need to make information clear, usable and responsible for the people delivering services. I would work in line with NHS Constitution duties around honesty, confidentiality, non-discrimination and professional accountability, and with the Trust’s CARE values: compassion by remembering that data supports real people and services; accountability through accurate, transparent reporting; respect through inclusive and non-blaming communication; and excellence by using information to support improvement.

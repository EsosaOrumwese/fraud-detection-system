# Interview Evidence - Five Guys Role

## Prompt

Alright, this would be like an interview. You are the interviewee and I am the interviewer and your answer should be between 2500 words to 5000 without waffling but instead focusing on detail. If I notice it is vague, I increase the word count closer to 5000.

First question: Can you describe your role at Five Guys? What were your duties and responsibilities? How is your day-to-day like?

## Response

I’ll answer as Eric in first person.

My role at Five Guys is Crew Member. I have been working there since September 2024, and the day-to-day role is very hands-on. It is a frontline food-service and customer-service role, but it is also very process-driven. A normal shift involves serving customers, preparing food, working across different stations, keeping the store clean and safe, restocking, following food hygiene procedures, communicating with the team, and keeping standards consistent during busy periods.

At a simple level, my job is to help the store deliver orders accurately, safely and quickly. But in practice, that means paying attention to a lot of moving parts at the same time: customer orders, delivery orders, food preparation, grill timing, fries, toppings, stock levels, cleanliness, queue pressure, order accuracy and team communication. It is not a role where you can work in isolation. Every station affects the next one.

On a typical shift, the first thing I do is understand where I am needed. Depending on the shift, I may be placed on till, dressing, fries, grill support, lobby, prep, cleaning or restocking. The store can change quickly, so even if I start in one position, I may need to support another station when it gets busy. For example, I might begin by helping with prep or restocking, then move to dressing when orders start building up, then help fries or bagging during the rush. The job requires flexibility because the priority is always the flow of service.

One of my main duties is customer service. When I am on till or interacting with customers, I need to take orders clearly, listen carefully, answer questions and make sure the order is entered correctly. Five Guys orders can be detailed because customers choose toppings and combinations, so accuracy matters. A small mistake at the till can affect the whole order later. I need to confirm details, especially when customers customise burgers, hot dogs, sandwiches, fries, drinks or shakes. If the store is busy, I still need to avoid rushing the customer in a way that causes errors.

Another part of customer service is handling questions or issues calmly. Customers may ask about ingredients, allergens, wait times, delivery collections, missing items or order changes. I am not there to argue or make assumptions. I need to listen, check what is happening, speak to a shift lead or manager when needed, and make sure the customer gets a clear answer. In a busy food environment, people can become frustrated if they are waiting, so tone matters. I have learned that staying calm and clear helps prevent small issues from becoming bigger ones.

Food preparation is another major part of the role. Before and during service, the team needs to make sure ingredients and stations are ready. That includes checking that toppings, buns, cheese, sauces, packaging, cups, napkins and other items are available. If a station runs out of something during a rush, it slows down the whole line. So part of the job is not just reacting when something is empty, but noticing early when something is getting low. For example, if toppings are running down or packaging is low, I need to restock before it becomes a problem for the person dressing orders or bagging food.

The dressing station requires concentration because it is where order details become visible. If a customer has asked for specific toppings, sauces or no certain ingredient, that needs to be followed exactly. It is easy for errors to happen if the person is not focused, especially when several tickets are waiting. I have to read the ticket carefully, prepare the item in the correct order, and communicate with the team if something is unclear. If I am unsure, it is better to check than to assume. That has taught me the importance of accuracy under pressure.

Fries is another area where timing and attention matter. Fries need to be cooked, seasoned, portioned and served properly, and the station can become intense when several orders are waiting. The person on fries needs to manage batches, avoid falling behind, and communicate with bagging or other stations. If fries are not ready, completed burgers may wait. If too many are cooked at the wrong time, quality can drop. So it is a balance of pace, quality and judgement. Even when I am not leading that station, I need to understand how my work affects it.

Cleaning and hygiene are continuous responsibilities, not just tasks at the end of the shift. Throughout the day, surfaces need to be wiped, spills dealt with, equipment and utensils handled correctly, bins managed, and customer areas kept presentable. Food hygiene is important because it affects customer safety, not just store appearance. I need to follow procedures around cleanliness, handwashing, gloves, cross-contamination awareness, and keeping work areas organised. If the store is busy, cleaning can be easy to delay, but the standard still has to be maintained.

A lot of the work is also about teamwork. During service, the team communicates constantly. Someone may call out that fries are needed, that an order is waiting on a particular item, that a delivery driver has arrived, that a customer has a question, or that stock is running low. If communication breaks down, orders slow down or mistakes happen. I have learned to give short, clear updates rather than vague ones. For example, instead of saying “we need more stuff,” it is better to say exactly what is needed: “we need more bags,” “we are low on cups,” “this order is waiting on fries,” or “ticket number X is ready to be bagged.”

The day-to-day environment can be fast and physically demanding. During peak times, there may be a queue in store, delivery-app orders coming through, customers waiting for collection, and several stations working at once. In those moments, it is important to stay focused on the immediate task while still being aware of the wider operation. If I am dressing an order, I need to focus on the ticket. If I am bagging, I need to check the order is complete. If I am on lobby, I need to keep the customer area clean and notice whether customers need help. The pace can be high, but quality cannot be sacrificed.

One of the most important things I have learned is that frontline service depends on small details being done correctly. For example, a missing item, wrong topping, unclear handover, late restock or messy station may seem small on its own, but during a rush it can create delays and frustration. That has made me more disciplined about checking my work. If an order is ready to go, I do not just assume it is complete because the bag looks full. I check the ticket, check the contents, and make sure the order is handed over properly.

My role also involves following standard procedures. Five Guys has a clear way of doing things, and the consistency of the brand depends on crew members following those standards. That includes how food is prepared, how stations are maintained, how customer service is handled, and how hygiene standards are met. I am expected to work within those procedures, not create my own shortcut because the store is busy. That has helped me build discipline around process and consistency.

A typical day can look different depending on whether it is an opening, mid, evening or closing shift. On an opening or earlier shift, there may be more preparation, checking stock, setting up stations and making sure the store is ready for service. During the middle of the day, the focus is usually speed, accuracy and keeping stations stocked through lunch demand. In the evening, there may be a mix of dine-in, takeaway and delivery orders, followed by cleaning, restocking and closing tasks. Closing requires attention to detail because the store has to be cleaned properly and left ready for the next day.

I have also become more aware of stock and preparation. In a food-service environment, operations can slow down if the team does not monitor what is running low. For example, if fries, toppings, cups, bags or cleaning supplies are not replenished early, someone has to leave their station during a busy period to fix it. So part of my day-to-day responsibility is noticing these things before they become urgent. That might mean restocking a station during a quieter moment, telling the shift lead when something is low, or helping another crew member who is about to run out of something.

The role also involves dealing with delivery orders and collections. Delivery orders create another layer of pressure because the customer is not physically there, but accuracy still matters. The order needs to be prepared, checked, packed and handed over correctly. If something is missed, it is harder to fix once the order has left the store. That reinforces the need for checking, clear ticket reading and good communication with colleagues.

From a responsibility point of view, I would not describe myself as a manager. I am a Crew Member, so I work within the team and under shift leads/managers. But I do take responsibility for my station and for supporting the wider shift. If I see that another station is under pressure and I can help, I try to do that. If something is wrong or unclear, I raise it rather than ignore it. If a customer needs help, I respond professionally. If a task needs finishing, I do not assume someone else will do it.

One thing I have developed in the role is resilience under pressure. In a busy shift, the pressure is immediate and visible. Customers are waiting, orders are on screens or tickets, colleagues are moving quickly, and mistakes can happen if people panic. I have learned to slow myself down mentally even when the environment is fast. That means reading the ticket properly, listening to instructions, checking before handing over, and communicating clearly. It is better to take a few extra seconds to get something right than to rush and cause a mistake that takes longer to fix.

I have also developed a better understanding of service from the customer’s side. Customers do not see every internal issue. They judge the experience by whether they were listened to, whether the order was correct, whether the store looked clean, whether the staff were respectful, and whether problems were handled properly. That has made me more aware that service work is not only about completing tasks. It is about the experience the person receives.

In terms of duties and responsibilities, I would summarise my role as: serving customers, taking and checking orders, preparing food according to standards, supporting different stations, maintaining cleanliness and hygiene, restocking supplies, supporting delivery order flow, communicating with the team, following procedures, and helping the shift run smoothly. The role requires attention to detail, speed, accuracy, teamwork and professionalism.

What I value about the role is that it has given me direct experience of a live operational environment. In data or analyst work, it is easy to talk about operations in an abstract way. At Five Guys, I see what operations means on the ground: queues, pressure, mistakes, handovers, stock levels, customer expectations, team coordination and the impact of small process failures. That has made me more practical in how I think about information and service delivery. Good information is only useful if it helps people act in a real environment.

So, overall, my role at Five Guys is not a technical data role, but it has strengthened important behaviours that are relevant to any service-focused organisation: accountability, attention to detail, communication, teamwork, following procedures, working under pressure, and understanding the importance of the person receiving the service. In a healthcare context, I would not claim that this gives me clinical or NHS data experience, but it does give me a strong appreciation of frontline service, standards, and the importance of doing work accurately because it affects other people.

# Interview Evidence - Five Guys Single Best Story

## Prompt

Having had a look at the job description, person specification, competencies, values and all that. Can you tell me of a single story that best embodies and encapsulates the relevant parts of those documents that you believe your experience in Five Guys exposed you to?

## Response

The single Five Guys story I would use is **a busy shift where order flow started breaking down because different parts of the service were moving at different speeds, and I helped stabilise the handover between stations so customers received clearer communication and more accurate orders.**

I would not use Five Guys to pretend I have NHS analytics or healthcare reporting experience. For this job, Five Guys is strongest for the parts of the person specification around working flexibly, responding to pressure and change, improving small processes, communicating clearly, supporting frontline service, teamwork, accountability, respect and service quality. The Trust is looking for someone who can support frontline clinicians and services professionally, work with people, communicate clearly, and demonstrate CARE values — compassion, accountability, respect and excellence.

So the story I would choose is this.

In Five Guys, one of the busiest and most pressurised points of a shift is when in-store orders, takeaway orders and delivery-app orders all build up at the same time. On one shift, we had a rush where the problem was not just the number of orders, but the fact that different parts of the process were moving at different speeds. Some burgers were ready but waiting for fries. Some delivery orders had hot food ready but were still waiting on drinks or shakes. Some bags had been started but were not complete. Customers were asking for updates at the counter, delivery drivers were waiting, and the team was trying to keep moving without handing out the wrong order.

My role that day was not to manage the shift. I was a Crew Member, so I was working within the team and following the shift lead’s direction. But I could see that the immediate risk was accuracy. If we only tried to move faster, we could easily make things worse: a bag could go out missing fries, a delivery driver could take the wrong order, or a customer could be told their order was ready when it was actually still waiting on one item. In that kind of environment, a small handover mistake can create more work for the whole team because someone then has to remake, check, apologise or correct the order.

The first thing I did was focus on making the order status clearer. I started paying close attention to the tickets and the partially completed bags. Instead of treating every order in the same way, I mentally separated them into simple groups: orders ready to bag, orders waiting on fries, orders waiting on grill items, orders waiting on drinks or shakes, and delivery orders where the courier was already present. That sounds simple, but during a rush it matters because if the team does not know the status of each order, people end up checking the same bag repeatedly or asking the same question several times.

I also changed the way I communicated with colleagues. I tried not to use vague phrases like “this one is not ready” or “we need this order.” I used short, specific callouts, for example: “This ticket is waiting on fries,” “This delivery order still needs the shake,” or “This bag has burgers but no fries yet.” That helped the person on fries, the person on bagging, and the person dealing with customers know exactly what the blockage was. I was not making a new official process, but I was making the handover clearer in the moment.

Where I was involved in bagging or checking orders, I made sure I used the ticket as the source of truth. I checked the burger or sandwich, fries, drinks, shakes and any delivery labels before handover. In a rush, there is a temptation to assume that because an order looks complete, it is complete. I tried to avoid that. If the ticket said there should be fries and they were not in the bag yet, I did not treat the order as ready. If a drink or shake was still outstanding, I kept that visible rather than letting the bag disappear into the wrong pile.

I also helped with customer and courier communication. When people are waiting, silence makes the situation worse. A customer or delivery driver may not need a long explanation, but they do need a clear and respectful update. So where appropriate, I gave simple updates like, “That order is just waiting on fries,” or “We are finishing the shake for that order.” I avoided making promises I could not control, but I tried to make sure people understood that the order had not been forgotten. That is important because even when the delay is short, the customer experience is worse if nobody explains what is happening.

At the same time, I kept an eye on practical blockers around the station. During a rush, packaging, bags, cups, napkins or other supplies running low can slow the whole line down. When I had a small gap, I helped restock what was running low so that the person bagging or serving did not have to stop at the worst possible moment. Again, it was not a dramatic action, but in a live service environment small delays build on each other. If the team has to keep stopping to find bags, cups or lids, the queue feels worse and the chance of mistakes increases.

The result was that the shift became more controlled. I would not claim that I transformed the store or created a formal new process. That would be overstating it. But in that period, the team had a clearer view of which orders were actually ready and which were still waiting on something. It reduced repeated checking, helped prevent incomplete handovers, and gave customers and drivers clearer updates. For me, the main result was that we protected accuracy and service quality during pressure rather than just trying to move faster.

The reason this story matters for the Data & Insights Analyst role is that it shows something I think is very transferable: in any service environment, people need reliable status information to make good decisions. At Five Guys, that might mean knowing whether an order is ready, waiting on fries, or missing a drink. In a healthcare data role, the environment is obviously much more serious, but the principle is similar: if information is unclear, incomplete or poorly communicated, frontline teams can make decisions based on the wrong picture.

That shift also taught me that service improvement does not always begin with a big project. Sometimes it begins with seeing where confusion is happening and making the process clearer for the people doing the work. The Trust’s job description talks about supporting operational management, helping staff use information, communicating clearly with non-technical audiences, working flexibly, improving processes and supporting services. My Five Guys story does not prove the technical side of that, but it does show the frontline behaviour behind it: staying calm, making the status of work clearer, communicating accurately, supporting colleagues and keeping the end user in mind.

It also reflects the CARE values in a practical way. **Compassion** was shown by recognising that customers waiting without information can become frustrated, so I gave clear updates rather than ignoring them. **Accountability** was shown by not handing over orders unless I had checked them properly against the ticket. **Respect** was shown in how I communicated with customers, delivery drivers and colleagues, especially when people were under pressure. **Excellence** was shown by trying to maintain standards and improve the flow of work rather than accepting confusion as normal.

So if I had to choose one Five Guys story for this application, I would choose this one because it does not try to make Five Guys into a data analyst job. It uses the experience for what it genuinely proves: frontline service awareness, pressure, accuracy, teamwork, communication, process discipline and accountability. Those are relevant because this NHS role is not only about technical analysis. It is also about producing information that helps real services work better, and doing that with responsibility, clarity and respect for the people affected by the work.

# Interview Evidence - South Western Technologies Role

## Question

That's good. Alright, looking at your experience, you also mentioned working as a Data Analyst at South Western Technologies & Oil Field Services Ltd? What does the company do, as I'm guessing it's hands-on oil and gas rigs, completions? And what were your responsibilities in such an active company? How was your day to day like? What were your expected outcomes, etc?

## Response

I’ll answer as Eric in first person. Treat the operational details as a concrete candidate-profile draft that should be confirmed against what actually happened.

Yes. South Western Technologies & Oilfield Services Ltd was an oilfield services company, so the environment was very different from a normal office or a purely corporate data role. It was connected to upstream oil and gas operations: well intervention, pumping and cementing, completion, well stimulation, well testing, drill stem/TCP, gravel pack and pumping, and coiled tubing/slickline services. The company describes itself as an indigenous service provider to oil and gas exploration and production companies, with a focus on service delivery, quality, safety, timing and budget, and it also states that it applies a strong QHSE culture across its operations. ([swoilfield.com][1])

So when I say I worked there as a Data Analyst, I do not mean that I was sitting somewhere analysing abstract business data with no connection to operations. The data was tied to real service delivery: jobs, clients, crews, equipment, field activity, safety reporting, mobilisation, documentation, and close-out. The company had an operations base in Port Harcourt and a corporate headquarters in Lagos, so the data environment reflected both field activity and office reporting needs. ([swoilfield.com][2])

My role was to support operational and management reporting by turning activity records into clearer information for supervisors, managers and internal teams. I was not a field engineer, and I was not the person making technical well decisions on site. My work sat around the information that came out of those activities: what job was planned, what service line it belonged to, which client or field it related to, whether equipment or crew had been assigned, whether the job had started, whether it was completed, whether documentation had been returned, and whether anything was delayed or missing.

A typical day depended on what reporting cycle we were in. Some days were focused on updating operational trackers. Some days were focused on checking records for missing or inconsistent information. Some days involved preparing weekly or monthly reports for review. Other days involved chasing clarification from colleagues because the spreadsheet or report alone did not tell the full story. That was one of the first things I learned: in an active operations company, the data is only useful when it reflects what is actually happening on the ground.

One of my regular responsibilities was maintaining service activity reporting. The company had different types of work going on, such as intervention, pumping and cementing, well testing, completions or stimulation-related services. Each job could have a client, job number, service type, location, planned date, actual date, status, crew or equipment reference, and close-out status. My job was to help bring those records into a usable form so that management could see what had been completed, what was still active, what was delayed, and what was awaiting documentation.

For example, a job could be marked as completed in one tracker, but when I checked the supporting details, the service report might not yet have been returned, the equipment return might not have been confirmed, or the close-out documentation might still be incomplete. From a pure spreadsheet point of view, it might look finished. From an operational point of view, it was not fully closed. That distinction mattered because if managers thought a job was fully closed when it was still awaiting documentation, the next team might assume that equipment, personnel or paperwork was available when it was not.

So part of my day-to-day work was checking the difference between status and reality. I would look at job status fields, dates, service type, documentation notes, and sometimes compare one tracker against another. If one source said “completed” but another still had missing close-out items, I would flag that as an exception. The purpose was not to make the spreadsheet neat for its own sake. The purpose was to make sure the report gave managers a usable view of operations.

Another responsibility was helping with weekly reporting around job progress. A weekly operations report might need to show jobs completed that week, jobs still in progress, jobs delayed, and jobs awaiting further action. The useful part was not just counting jobs. It was grouping them in a way that answered management questions. For example: Which service lines had the most active work? Which jobs were delayed? Were delays connected to equipment availability, client approval, documentation, logistics or something else? Which completed jobs were not yet properly closed out?

In practice, that meant I had to work with records that were not always consistent. Sometimes the same client name could be written in different ways. Sometimes a job status could be too vague, such as “pending,” without saying whether it was pending approval, pending mobilisation, pending documents, or pending equipment release. Sometimes dates were missing or did not line up. Sometimes a job was updated in one place but not another. My work involved cleaning and standardising those records, but cleaning was only one part of it. The bigger responsibility was interpretation: understanding what the field meant in that operational context.

One concrete example was a close-out tracker I supported. After a field service job, several things needed to line up before the job could be treated as closed: the job number, client reference, service report, completion date, crew time or man-hour record, equipment return or demobilisation note, and any invoice-support documentation. If one of those was missing, the job might still require follow-up even if the field activity itself had ended.

I helped structure the tracker so that each job could be reviewed by status: completed and fully documented, completed but awaiting service report, completed but missing equipment return confirmation, delayed, cancelled, or still active. That made it easier for colleagues to see what needed action. Instead of someone reading through email threads or several spreadsheets to find what was missing, the tracker made the outstanding issue visible.

The expected outcome of that kind of work was clearer visibility. Managers needed to know what was really going on without having to inspect every raw record themselves. Operational colleagues needed to know what required follow-up. Admin or commercial teams needed to know which jobs were ready for the next stage. My report was useful only if it helped someone act.

Another area I was exposed to was equipment and mobilisation tracking. In oilfield services, equipment is not just an asset on a list. It determines whether a job can happen. If equipment is still tied to a previous job, awaiting inspection, missing documentation, or not ready for mobilisation, that affects planning. I worked around records showing which equipment was assigned, which jobs it was connected to, whether it had been mobilised or returned, and whether there were any outstanding issues.

A practical example would be where equipment was assumed to be available because the job had ended, but the return or inspection status had not been confirmed. If that was not picked up, the operations team could plan around equipment that was not actually ready. My role was to help make that kind of gap visible in the tracker. I would not claim I made engineering decisions about the equipment. I did not. But I helped organise the information so the people responsible for operations had a clearer picture.

QHSE reporting was also part of the type of environment I worked around. In a company like South Western, safety and quality are central because the services are field-based and operationally sensitive. The company itself emphasises QHSE culture. ([swoilfield.com][1]) That meant reporting could involve things like man-hours, incident or near-miss logs, toolbox talk attendance, site reports, observation cards, or compliance-related submissions.

From a data point of view, the challenge with QHSE information is that low numbers do not automatically mean there is no risk. If one team submits detailed observation cards and another submits almost nothing, you have to ask whether the second team had fewer observations or whether reporting discipline was weaker. That taught me a useful lesson about data interpretation: a number can be technically correct but still misleading if you do not understand how it was produced.

So when compiling or checking reports, I paid attention to completeness and consistency. If one month had missing submissions from a particular team or service line, that needed to be treated differently from a true zero. If man-hours were entered but supporting attendance records were missing, that needed checking. Again, the work was not only data cleaning. It was making sure the report did not create a false sense of certainty.

My expected outputs were usually practical reporting products: updated trackers, cleaned data tables, weekly summaries, exception lists, management-ready tables, charts, or supporting analysis for internal review. I used tools such as Excel heavily, and where needed I would work with structured tables, formulas, filters, lookups, pivot tables, charts and validation checks. The point was to make repeated reporting easier and less error-prone.

One thing I tried to improve was reducing repeated manual checking. In an environment where people are working across operations, admin and management, it is easy for reporting to become a copy-and-paste exercise. A person updates a spreadsheet manually, someone else checks another sheet, and then a third person asks why the numbers do not match. I helped make some of those reports more structured by standardising fields, making status categories clearer, and adding checks so that obvious gaps were easier to catch.

For example, if a job had a completed date but no close-out document status, that could be flagged. If a job was marked as active but had a completion date entered, that could be reviewed. If the same service type was written in three different ways, it could be standardised so the pivot table or report did not split one category into several. These are small changes, but they matter because they reduce confusion and make the report more reliable.

Day to day, I also had to communicate with people. That is an important part of the role because the data did not explain itself. If something was unclear, I might need to ask a colleague what a status meant, confirm whether a job had actually been completed, or check whether missing documentation had been received but not recorded. I learned not to assume that the spreadsheet had the full truth. Sometimes the record was out of date because the field team had moved faster than the admin update. Sometimes the opposite happened: something was recorded as complete, but the supporting document had not yet arrived.

That required careful communication. I had to ask questions in a way that did not sound like blame. For example, instead of saying, “This data is wrong,” it was better to say, “This job is marked completed, but the tracker does not show the service report as received. Has that been submitted, or should I leave it as awaiting documentation?” That kind of wording helped keep the focus on resolving the issue.

The most important outcome expected from me was not to produce complicated analysis. It was to produce information people could trust. In operational reporting, trust comes from accuracy, consistency and usefulness. If managers found that the report regularly contained mismatched statuses or missing details, they would stop relying on it. So I had to pay attention to detail and make sure that what I sent out or prepared was defensible.

The role also taught me how reporting supports decision-making. For example, a manager looking at a weekly activity report might use it to ask: Which jobs are taking longer than expected? Which service lines are busiest? Are we waiting on client approval, equipment availability or internal documentation? Which jobs are ready for closure? Which ones need escalation? A good report should help those questions become visible quickly.

I would describe my responsibilities in four broad areas.

First, data collection and consolidation. I worked with operational records from different sources, bringing them together into one place so they could be reviewed. That could include job trackers, activity reports, service records, close-out information or QHSE-related entries.

Second, data checking and validation. I checked for missing dates, inconsistent statuses, duplicated or mismatched job records, unclear service categories, incomplete close-out fields and totals that did not make sense. I learned that validation is not just a technical step; it is a business step because it protects people from making decisions based on an inaccurate view.

Third, reporting and visualisation. I helped turn the checked data into weekly or monthly summaries, tables, charts or trackers. These were not always sophisticated dashboards, but they were operationally useful. A clear table showing delayed jobs and reasons can be more valuable than a complicated visual that nobody uses.

Fourth, communication and follow-up. I helped explain what the report was showing, flagged gaps, and asked for clarification when records were incomplete. I had to make sure that the information was understandable to colleagues who were not data specialists.

A normal day might start with checking the latest updates to the operational tracker. I would look for new jobs added, status changes, dates updated, documentation received, and any outstanding items from the previous report. Then I might clean or standardise entries, update pivot tables or summary views, and check whether totals matched the source records. If something looked wrong, I would note it and ask the relevant person for clarification. Later, I might prepare a summary for a manager or update a report for review.

During busier reporting periods, the work was more deadline-driven. If a weekly report was needed for an operations meeting, I had to make sure the information was updated in time. That meant prioritising the most important checks first: statuses, dates, delayed jobs, completed jobs and missing close-out items. Less critical formatting could wait. The purpose was to give managers a reliable picture before the meeting.

The environment also helped me understand that reporting has consequences. If a job is shown as delayed, someone may ask why. If a job is shown as ready for close-out, someone may start the next process. If a safety or QHSE figure is incomplete, management may not see the real level of reporting activity. So I had to be careful not to present uncertain information as certain. Where something was unclear, I learned to mark it clearly rather than hide it.

That experience is very relevant to how I think about data now. It taught me that operational data is messy because operations are real. People are moving between sites, equipment is being mobilised, jobs change, documents come in late, and different teams update different records. The analyst’s job is not to complain that the data is imperfect. The job is to understand the process well enough to make the information usable, while being honest about gaps and limitations.

The role also developed my ability to work between technical and non-technical people. I might be using formulas, checks or structured reporting behind the scenes, but the person reading the report usually wanted a clear answer: What happened? What is delayed? What is missing? What needs attention? That is similar to how I understand good data and insight work generally. The technical work matters, but the final output must help the user make a better decision.

I would not overstate the role. I was not managing major oilfield projects, and I was not leading field operations. But as a Data Analyst, I was contributing to the information layer that supported those operations. My work helped convert scattered operational records into clearer reports, highlighted missing or inconsistent information, supported follow-up, and made recurring reporting more reliable.

If I connect that to the type of Data & Insights Analyst role I am applying for now, the relevance is that I have seen how data supports active services. In South Western, the service context was oilfield operations. In the NHS, the context would be patient care, clinical services, operational management and service improvement. The subject matter is different, and I would need to learn NHS systems and governance. But the underlying discipline is familiar: understand the process, check whether the data reflects reality, identify gaps, produce clear insight, and communicate it in a way that helps people act.

## Anchor story - South Western Technologies & Oilfield Services Ltd

### Situation

When I worked as a Data Analyst at South Western Technologies & Oilfield Services Ltd, one of the recurring problems was that operations information existed in several places, but managers needed one clear view before weekly planning and close-out discussions.

The company was involved in oilfield services such as well intervention, pumping/cementing, completions, stimulation and testing. So the data was not abstract. It related to real jobs: client work orders, service lines, field locations, crew assignment, equipment movement, job status, mobilisation, demobilisation, service reports and close-out documentation.

A typical issue was that a job could look “completed” in one tracker, but it was not truly closed from an operational or commercial point of view. For example, the field activity may have ended, but the signed service report might still be missing. Or the job might have a completion date, but the equipment return had not been confirmed. Or the crew man-hours were submitted, but the close-out document was still blank. If that was not visible, managers could make decisions from an incomplete picture.

The problem was not just messy data. The problem was that scattered data made it harder to know:

* which jobs were completed;
* which jobs were still active;
* which jobs were delayed;
* which jobs were blocked by missing documentation;
* which equipment was still tied to a job;
* which records needed follow-up before the next stage.

### Task

My task was to help turn those scattered operational records into a clearer weekly reporting pack.

The expected outcome was not a complicated model. The expected outcome was a practical management view that could answer:

> “What is happening across jobs this week, what is delayed, what is missing, and what needs action?”

I needed to produce something reliable enough for managers and operational colleagues to use without manually checking every spreadsheet, email thread or job record themselves.

### Action

The first thing I did was identify the main data sources that fed into the report. These included the job activity tracker, service/job report records, equipment or mobilisation notes, crew/man-hour records, and close-out documentation status.

I then built a structured Excel workbook around the fields that actually mattered for weekly reporting. The key fields included:

* client name;
* job number;
* service line;
* field or location;
* planned start date;
* actual start date;
* completion date;
* crew or team assigned;
* equipment package or unit assigned;
* job status;
* delay reason;
* signed service report received;
* equipment return confirmed;
* man-hour record complete;
* close-out status;
* outstanding action.

The important part was that the workbook was not just a list of jobs. It was structured so the data could be grouped, filtered and checked.

I used Excel tools such as structured tables, filters, pivot tables, lookup formulas, conditional formatting and validation checks. Where the same service line or client name was written in different ways, I standardised the categories so that the report would not split one service line into three different labels. For example, if the same type of service was written with slightly different wording, I normalised it into one reporting category.

I also created exception checks. These were the most useful part of the work. The workbook flagged records such as:

* job marked “completed” but no service report received;
* job marked “active” but completion date already entered;
* completion date present but close-out status blank;
* equipment still assigned to a completed job;
* missing delay reason for a delayed job;
* duplicated job number;
* service line missing or unclear;
* man-hours submitted but not linked clearly to the job.

That changed the report from a passive spreadsheet into a working control document. Instead of only showing totals, it showed where attention was needed.

I also used pivot tables and charts to prepare a simple weekly summary. The summary showed:

* number of active jobs;
* number of completed jobs;
* number of delayed jobs;
* delayed jobs by reason;
* jobs awaiting service reports;
* jobs awaiting equipment return confirmation;
* jobs ready for close-out;
* jobs blocked by missing records.

This meant the operations discussion could start from the exception list instead of people searching line by line through raw records.

I was also careful not to treat every blank or zero as the same thing. For example, if a service line had no incidents or no delays recorded, that could mean there were genuinely no issues, or it could mean the update had not been submitted. So I separated “none reported” from “not submitted” where possible. That helped avoid a misleading report.

Communication was a big part of the work. If a record looked wrong, I did not just mark it as wrong. I went back to the relevant person with a specific question. For example:

> “This job is marked as completed, but the tracker does not show the signed service report. Has the report been received, or should I leave it as awaiting documentation?”

That wording mattered because it kept the conversation focused on resolving the gap, not blaming someone for bad data.

Once the weekly report was prepared, the output could be used by managers and operational colleagues to see what needed follow-up. The report made it easier to identify whether a delay was caused by documentation, equipment availability, client approval, logistics, or another issue.

### Result

The result was a clearer weekly view of operations.

I would not overstate it and say I transformed the whole company. But the reporting pack made the job status picture easier to understand. It reduced repeated manual checking, made missing documentation more visible, and helped colleagues see which jobs needed follow-up before they could be treated as fully closed.

The most useful output was the exception list. It gave managers a focused view of the jobs that needed attention rather than forcing them to inspect every record. It also helped operational and admin colleagues understand what was blocking close-out: missing service report, unconfirmed equipment return, incomplete man-hour record, unclear delay reason, or inconsistent status.

For me, the biggest result was that the report became more decision-oriented. It did not just say “here is the data.” It said:

> “Here are the jobs that are fine, here are the jobs that need attention, and here is why.”

### Reflection

This experience taught me that good data analysis is not just about technical skill. The tools matter, but the purpose matters more.

I used Excel, structured reporting tables, lookups, pivot tables, validation checks, conditional formatting and summary charts. But the reason those tools mattered was because they helped turn scattered operational records into information that managers could trust and act on.

It also taught me three things that are directly relevant to the Data & Insights Analyst role.

First, data has to be checked against the real process. A job marked “completed” is not always operationally closed. In the NHS, I imagine the equivalent could be a patient pathway, service report, contract return or performance measure that looks complete in one system but needs careful interpretation before it is used for decision-making.

Second, data quality is not a side task. If statuses, dates, categories or definitions are inconsistent, the final report can mislead people. That is why I built checks into the workbook and separated genuine values from missing information.

Third, communication is part of analysis. The report only worked because the gaps were explained clearly and because I asked operational colleagues specific questions they could answer. That fits the kind of role where analysts need to translate technical or data issues into language that service managers and non-technical users can use.

For this NHS role, I would use this story because it shows the closest match from my South Western experience: multiple data sources, operational reporting, data quality checks, repeatable reporting, reducing manual review, and explaining exceptions to colleagues. It does not claim direct NHS experience, but it shows the same discipline the Trust is asking for: producing accurate, meaningful information that supports operational decisions.

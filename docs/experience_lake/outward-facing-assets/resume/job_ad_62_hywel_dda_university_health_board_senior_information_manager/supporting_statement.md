# Supporting Statement

I am applying for the Senior Information Manager role because my experience sits across operational reporting, data quality, information assurance, stakeholder communication and the development of reliable reporting outputs. I understand this post is about supporting a corporate information management framework, not simply producing tables. The role requires accurate internal and external reporting, clear definitions, delivery to timescales, support for clinical and managerial users, and improvement of reporting systems so that information supports accountability and service improvement.

I meet the formal knowledge requirements through a First Class BEng Mechanical Engineering degree from Covenant University and an MSc Data Science with Artificial Intelligence from the University of Exeter, completed with Distinction. My postgraduate training strengthened my ability to work with complex datasets, assess data quality, choose appropriate analytical methods and explain limitations clearly. My research work involved high-volume sensor data, preprocessing, modelling and evaluation, which developed the discipline to question whether data is representative, whether definitions are clear and whether an output is safe to use for decisions. I have continued to build my capability through applied projects, Power BI reporting, SQL reporting work, Microsoft Access, Excel automation, data protection learning and targeted preparation around NHS data flows, data dictionary concepts and national reporting definitions.

My strongest workplace evidence comes from my Data Analyst role at South Western Technologies & Oilfield Services Ltd, where I supported recurring reports across service activity, equipment movement, QHSE, man-hours, close-out documentation and management information. The work involved Oracle data extracts as structured operational sources, SAS programs for repeatable analytical processing, Excel and Access for reporting inputs and checks, and Power BI for presenting trends, status views and exceptions. I produced reporting outputs showing which jobs were active, delayed, field-completed, awaiting documentation, awaiting equipment confirmation or ready for close-out. This gave managers a clearer view of operational status and helped teams understand what required action.

A common reporting issue was that a job could be shown as completed in one source but still be missing a signed service report, equipment return confirmation, man-hour record or close-out status. I used Oracle outputs, SAS transformation steps, Access tables and Excel checks to separate raw extracts, cleaned datasets, exception views and final reporting outputs. I checked row counts, duplicate records, missing fields, inconsistent status logic, unclear service categories, failed joins and unexpected movements between reporting periods. This gave me practical experience of reporting assurance: a report is only useful if the source data, definitions, transformations and assumptions have been checked.

I have strong experience with the Microsoft and analytical tools named in the person specification. In Excel, I have built structured reporting workbooks, pivot tables, lookups, formulas, conditional formatting, validation checks, exception lists and management-ready summaries, and used macros and VBA to automate repeated workbook checks, formatting and report-preparation steps. In Access, I have used tables, queries and controlled local data structures where a spreadsheet alone was not robust enough for recurring reporting inputs. In Power BI, I have produced dashboards showing trends, exceptions, status views and data-quality measures. I have also used SQL to create reporting queries, joins, validation checks and curated outputs for dashboards and analysis, alongside Python, SAS and Oracle for data preparation and testing.

A key strength I would bring is using information to present a cogent argument. At South Western, managers did not need a raw extract; they needed to know which jobs were ready, which were blocked, what was causing delay and who needed to act. One useful improvement was separating "field-completed" from "ready for close-out," because one broad "completed" category hid important follow-up work. That distinction helped discussions move from general disagreement about status to specific actions: chase the report, confirm equipment return, clarify the delay reason or keep the job on the exception list.

Data quality and data standards are central to how I work. In QHSE reporting, I learned not to treat missing submissions as true zero values. A service line with low recorded incidents might reflect low risk, but it might also reflect incomplete reporting. I used rates such as observations per 1,000 man-hours, trend views, standard deviation, median and mean comparisons, Pareto breakdowns and completeness checks to improve interpretation. I also applied confidence intervals, ANOVA and statistical process control in postgraduate and project work to compare groups and judge whether movement over time reflected normal variation or a change needing investigation. The main lesson I would bring is that figures must be interpreted in context before they are used to support decisions.

My reporting background has required me to turn rules into practical checks: defining what is included, what is excluded, which source is authoritative, which fields need validation, and how uncertainty should be shown before a report is treated as final. I understand that in this role, national guidance and Welsh Government targets would need to be translated into practical reporting rules: inclusion and exclusion criteria, source selection, validation checks, refresh timing, sign-off evidence and clear notes on limitations. I would apply that discipline to Hywel Dda's national reporting requirements, local definitions, data flows, targets and corporate reporting standards.

I can also evidence reporting system development and documentation through my independent AWS fraud and transaction decisioning platform. I would not present this as the main evidence for this role, but it strengthened my approach to reporting assurance. I documented validation rules, dashboard measures, data lineage and monitoring checks so that a change in the data flow could be distinguished from a real change in activity. That experience is relevant to maintaining reliable reports, protecting definitions, checking dashboard measures and ensuring that reporting outputs remain traceable.

I understand the importance of information governance, confidentiality, records management and safe release of information in a Health Board role. My working understanding is that data must be used lawfully, fairly, securely and proportionately, with clear purpose, minimum necessary access, accurate definitions, appropriate retention and documented accountability. For information requests, I would check definitions, date ranges, exclusions, validation evidence, small-number disclosure risk and whether figures should be aggregated before treating them as ready to share. In my platform work, I separated raw events from curated analytical outputs, restricted access to sensitive layers, avoided hard-coded credentials, documented lineage and made validation failures visible.

Communication and influence are among my strongest transferable areas. At South Western, I presented reporting exceptions through written summaries, dashboard views and verbal explanations, adapting the level of detail depending on whether I was speaking to operations, QHSE, admin, commercial colleagues or managers. These colleagues were not under my line management, so I had to influence through evidence, clarity and respect. I avoided blaming language and translated data issues into practical questions, such as whether a service report had been received, whether equipment had returned, or whether a delay related to client approval, logistics, documentation or internal follow-up. This helped colleagues correct records, clarify definitions and take action.

I can also evidence training, mentoring and task-coordination responsibilities. At South Western, I supported NYSC students and industrial training students by explaining reporting workbooks, setting small checking tasks, reviewing their checks, correcting misunderstandings around definitions and helping them understand how exceptions should be interpreted before outputs were used. In my current Crew Member role, I train new starters on station tasks, order accuracy, handovers and operating procedures, which has strengthened my ability to break tasks down, check understanding and correct errors patiently. These examples support the role's requirements around training, mentoring, team working and helping others use information more effectively.

I am comfortable working independently within a framework, managing competing demands and maintaining attention to detail under pressure. At South Western, I worked to recurring reporting deadlines while handling ad-hoc clarifications and unresolved data issues. I had to prioritise the most important checks first, separate confirmed information from items requiring clarification, and communicate where deadlines or data quality issues affected confidence in the output. During my MSc and platform work, I also managed complex workloads over sustained periods, balancing technical delivery, documentation, testing and written outputs. I am also able to travel between Health Board sites in a timely manner and would take a flexible approach to service needs, including hybrid and site-based working where required.

I would bring to Hywel Dda University Health Board a strong combination of reporting discipline, data-quality judgement, analytical depth and people-focused communication. I am used to learning operational systems by tracing how fields, statuses, definitions, source records and validation checks affect final reporting. I would apply that same discipline to Health Board corporate reporting, national guidance, local definitions and performance requirements. I would work with dignity, respect and fairness when influencing colleagues; with integrity, openness and honesty when handling data and uncertainty; and with a focus on developing excellent services by making information accurate, clear and useful for the people who rely on it.

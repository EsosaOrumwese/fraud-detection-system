# Passed NHS Supporting Statement Patterns

Source:

- Local reference PDFs and template supplied by the user in `C:\Users\LEGION\Downloads\NHS Application draft`.
- These were reviewed as examples of applications/supporting statements that reportedly passed NHS sift.

This file extracts writing patterns only. It does not copy the source statements and should not be used as a wording template.

## Core Finding

The passed examples are not necessarily elegant. Some are repetitive, overlong, grammatically uneven, or too broad.

Their useful lesson is simpler:

> NHS sift writing works when the scorer can quickly see the criterion, the setting, the tool or method, the output, and why it mattered.

The goal is not polished essay writing. The goal is easy marking.

## Patterns Worth Keeping

### 1. The role and organisation are named early

Most examples open by naming the exact role and Trust or organisation. This signals that the statement was written for the post, not reused unchanged.

Use this lightly. One short opening is enough.

Good pattern:

> I am applying for this role because it matches my experience in [core job requirement] and my interest in supporting [service/user/patient/operational outcome].

Avoid long motivation before evidence.

### 2. The examples use recognisable work settings

The stronger statements do not only say "I have data skills." They place the evidence inside a recognisable setting:

- current role
- named type of system
- reporting process
- dashboard build
- data pipeline
- audit or validation process
- business or service problem
- operational users

For our materials, this means the open-source platform must be explained as a serious working environment with data flows, controls, reporting outputs, and decision needs. Do not leave it as an abstract project.

### 3. Tools are tied to actions

The examples mention tools such as SQL, Power BI, Excel, Python, SSIS, Azure, Power Query, DAX, Tableau, and dashboards, but the better sections attach the tool to a task.

Good pattern:

> I used SQL to extract and join the data, Power Query to automate the refresh, and Power BI to present the results.

Weak pattern:

> I am skilled in SQL, Excel, Power BI, Python, Tableau and dashboards.

Tool lists are weaker than tool use.

### 4. Outputs are concrete

The passed examples often mention concrete outputs:

- automated reports
- dashboards
- weekly/monthly reporting
- data models
- SQL views
- audit checks
- training material
- visualisations
- handover documentation

This matters because the scorer can see what was produced.

Our statements should always answer:

- What did I build, analyse, validate, automate, report, or document?
- Who or what was it for?
- What changed because of it?

### 5. Outcomes are practical, not only technical

The stronger examples link technical work to operational effect:

- reduced manual reporting time
- improved visibility
- supported managers
- reduced waiting or delay
- improved reporting accuracy
- enabled better decisions
- helped users adopt the tool

This is the missing bridge in weaker AI-like writing. The outcome should not be a vague "impact"; it should say what became easier, faster, clearer, safer, more accurate, or more usable.

### 6. NHS language is used, but usually in plain English

The passed examples use NHS-relevant words such as:

- patient care
- service improvement
- clinical and operational teams
- data governance
- national standards and definitions
- confidentiality
- routine and ad hoc reporting
- dashboards
- data quality
- decision-making

Use these only where defensible. Do not invent NHS exposure.

For adjacent evidence, prefer truthful phrasing:

> This gives me directly relevant experience in governed reporting, validation, documentation, and explaining data limitations, which I would apply carefully in an NHS setting.

Do not write:

> I have NHS clinical data experience.

unless that is true.

### 7. Short paragraphs help marking

The examples are easier to follow when each paragraph does one job:

- opening fit
- reporting/dashboard evidence
- data quality/governance evidence
- stakeholder or communication evidence
- technical tools evidence
- closing motivation

Long flowing statements become harder to score. A paragraph should usually answer one cluster of criteria.

### 8. Some repetition is acceptable, but uncontrolled repetition weakens the statement

Passed examples repeat role keywords like data quality, reporting, dashboards, stakeholders, SQL, and Power BI. This can help scoring.

But repetition should be controlled. Repeat the criterion language enough to be markable, not so much that the statement feels padded.

### 9. The employment history can carry evidence, but the supporting statement still needs to score

Several applications had detailed employment-history entries and then used the supporting statement to reinforce the strongest examples.

For us:

- CV/resume can carry the fast-scan proof.
- Supporting statement must still explicitly answer the criteria.
- Do not assume the scorer will connect the CV to the statement without help.

### 10. Human-written does not mean informal

The passed statements are direct, first-person, and sometimes imperfect. That makes them feel human. But they are still professional and criteria-focused.

Use natural first-person wording:

- `I used...`
- `I built...`
- `I checked...`
- `I produced...`
- `I documented...`
- `This meant...`

Avoid agent-like wording:

- `public-purpose link`
- `X-style`
- `production-shaped`
- `role-native spine`
- `stakeholder-readable`
- `governance-conscious`
- `decision-ready`

## Patterns Not Worth Copying

Do not copy these weaknesses from the examples:

- long introductions before evidence
- generic passion statements
- claims not attached to a specific situation
- excessive lists of tools
- repeated claims with no new evidence
- vague "I align perfectly" wording
- inflated phrases like "seasoned professional" if the evidence does not support it
- unsupported claims of clinical/NHS exposure
- overlong paragraphs that bury the action and result

Some passed examples likely succeeded because the candidate had direct role experience, not because every sentence was strong.

## Recommended NHS Statement Workflow

Before drafting the statement:

1. List the essential and desirable criteria.
2. Group overlapping criteria into paragraph clusters.
3. Decide the one-sentence story of the application.
4. Select the best evidence for each cluster.
5. Identify truth boundaries.
6. Set an internal word target below the employer maximum.

Then write using this paragraph pattern:

> I meet this requirement through [specific evidence area]. In [setting], I [specific action using tool/method]. This produced [output/result], which mattered because [service/reporting/quality/user outcome].

Not every paragraph needs to be full STAR, but every paragraph needs:

- criterion signal
- specific context
- action
- output or result
- relevance to the NHS role

## Default NHS Supporting Statement Shape

Use this only as a planning guide, not a rigid template.

1. Short opening: role fit and reason for applying.
2. Main technical evidence: reporting, SQL/Python/Excel/BI, dashboards, data models, pipelines, or analysis.
3. Data quality and governance: validation, reconciliation, confidentiality, documentation, standards, limitations.
4. Communication and users: explaining findings, supporting users, translating requests, producing usable outputs.
5. Delivery and personal attributes: prioritisation, accuracy, initiative, pressure, improvement.
6. Closing: concise statement of contribution and motivation.

For senior roles, add:

- project ownership
- quality assurance
- mentoring or knowledge sharing if true
- service improvement
- risk/escalation
- strategic or operational relevance

## Final NHS Sift Check

Before finalising, ask:

- Can a scorer tick each essential criterion without searching?
- Does every paragraph contain a real example or concrete evidence?
- Are tools connected to actions and outputs?
- Are outcomes practical and service-relevant?
- Is the NHS wording truthful rather than borrowed?
- Is the statement shorter than the employer maximum unless detail is genuinely needed?
- Does it sound like a person explaining real work?
- Have all constructed labels and agent shorthand been removed?

# Flagship Brief Strategy

## Position

The advanced analytics programme should be built around a small number of serious flagship briefs, not a catalogue of thin analytical slices.

The purpose is to emulate the work of an analytics function inside an operating organisation. In that setting, teams do not usually receive isolated tasks such as "use SQL," "build a dashboard," or "show governance." They receive operating problems. Solving those problems requires several capabilities to work together.

## What This Means

Each flagship brief should be rich enough to support multiple professional discussions about the same piece of work:

- how the stakeholder problem was understood
- how the data surfaces were selected and validated
- how SQL and Python were used to inspect and structure the evidence
- how metrics, denominators, and grains were controlled
- how statistical or modelling choices were made
- how data quality and realism concerns were handled
- how the result was communicated through visual evidence, reports, or BI
- how limitations were explained without weakening the value of the work
- how the final recommendation supported a business or operational decision

The target is not to map one brief to one job requirement. The target is to create briefs that are strong enough that, when discussed, they naturally demonstrate advanced analytics, BI, data quality, stakeholder engagement, operational judgement, governance, and communication together.

## Portfolio Shape

The preferred portfolio is two or three flagship briefs.

That is enough to provide range without diluting the work. A small number of deep briefs is more defensible than many shallow projects because it allows each one to carry:

- a credible stakeholder trigger
- a serious problem statement
- multiple data surfaces
- investigation and modelling where appropriate
- dashboard/reporting candidates
- assurance and caveat handling
- a final stakeholder-facing recommendation

Supporting briefs can exist, but they should only be created when they serve a flagship brief or resolve a sharply bounded issue. They should not become the main programme.

## Competition Standard

The work should be designed for a competitive hiring environment.

That does not mean distorting the work around specific job ads. It means maintaining a professional standard high enough that the delivered briefs would sound credible across public-sector, NHS, civil-service, operational analytics, BI, data science, and data-governance roles.

For a flagship brief to meet that standard, it should be possible to discuss it as:

- a stakeholder requirement-gathering example
- a technical SQL/Python analysis example
- a data-quality and assurance example
- a dashboard or reporting design example
- a model, metric, or statistical judgement example
- a communication and recommendation example
- a business-impact or operational-improvement example

If a brief cannot support several of those conversations, it may still be useful, but it is not a flagship brief.

## Guardrails

Do not design briefs as job-ad tick boxes.

Do not split capabilities artificially across briefs.

Do not create many small projects just to appear broad.

Do not treat dashboards as the final form of every brief. Some briefs may end in an analytical report, model evaluation memo, data-fitness judgement, monitoring design, or operational recommendation.

Do not hide caveats to make the work look stronger. For serious analytical work, the ability to state what the data can and cannot support is part of the evidence of competence.

## Practical Implication

When candidate briefs are drafted, the first review question should be:

> Is this a real operating problem that can carry a full analytical delivery arc?

If the answer is no, the brief should either be merged into a larger flagship brief, converted into a supporting task, or rejected.
